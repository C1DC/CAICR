# Module 2 — Institutional Cyber Risk & Digital Resilience

**Day 1 · Afternoon · Leadership module (no Kali lab — you build a risk register)**

In one sentence: cyber risk is not an "IT problem" — it is business risk that
happens to involve computers, and this module teaches you to measure it, own it,
and decide what to do about it.

By the end you will be able to:
- express a cyber threat as a clear business-risk statement;
- score risk by likelihood and impact, and tell inherent from residual risk;
- place controls against the six NIST CSF 2.0 functions;
- build a risk register that a board would actually act on.

---

## The big picture

A **risk** is the chance that something bad happens *and* how much it would hurt.
In cyber, the "something bad" is a threat exploiting a weakness. We write it as a
sentence anyone can understand:

> IF *[a threat]* exploits *[a vulnerability]*, THEN the institution faces
> *[a business consequence]*.

For example: *IF an attacker exploits our unpatched remote-access server, THEN
citizen records could be stolen and we face DPC penalties and public trust
loss.* Notice the consequence is in **business terms** — money, trust, legal,
safety — not "a server gets owned". Boards fund consequences they understand.

Two numbers turn a scenario into a comparable risk:

- **Likelihood** — how probable is it? (e.g., 1–5)
- **Impact** — how bad if it happens? (e.g., 1–5)

Multiply (or plot on a grid) and you get a **risk rating** you can rank against
every other risk. That ranking is the whole point: you cannot fix everything, so
you fix the biggest risks first.

Then distinguish two states of every risk:

- **Inherent risk** — how bad it is with *no controls*.
- **Residual risk** — what's *left* after your controls are in place.

The gap between them is the value your controls deliver. Residual risk is what
leadership must consciously **accept, or reduce further**.

## An analogy

Think of insuring a building against fire. You don't start by buying a sprinkler
for every cupboard. You ask: how likely is a fire, and how bad would it be? A
paper archive (high impact) near an old fuse box (high likelihood) jumps to the
top. You then choose: install sprinklers (**mitigate**), buy insurance
(**transfer**), stop storing paper there (**avoid**), or decide the risk is
small enough to live with and write that decision down (**accept**). Cyber risk
treatment is exactly these four choices.

## How it actually works

The four **risk treatments** — memorise them:

- **Mitigate** — add controls to reduce likelihood or impact (patch, MFA,
  backups).
- **Transfer** — shift the cost to someone else (cyber insurance, a contract
  clause).
- **Avoid** — stop doing the risky thing (decommission the exposed service).
- **Accept** — knowingly live with it — but *only* with a named owner and a
  review date. **Acceptance without accountability is negligence.**

Controls are organised by the **NIST CSF 2.0** framework — six functions that
cover the whole lifecycle:

| Function | Plain meaning | Example |
|----------|---------------|---------|
| **GOVERN** | Who's accountable; strategy & policy | Board approves a cyber-risk policy and budget |
| **IDENTIFY** | Know your assets and risks | A current asset inventory exists |
| **PROTECT** | Put up safeguards | MFA on all accounts; staff trained |
| **DETECT** | Notice when something's wrong | SIEM alerts reviewed daily |
| **RESPOND** | Act on an incident | A tested incident plan; who calls whom |
| **RECOVER** | Get back to normal, learn | Backups tested monthly |

GOVERN is new in CSF 2.0 and it is the big message: cybersecurity is now a
**board-level duty**, not something delegated to "the IT guy".

Finally, two service-level numbers leaders must set *per critical service*:

- **MTD / RTO** — the longest a service can be down before real harm (recovery
  *time* objective).
- **MADL / RPO** — the most data you can afford to lose (recovery *point*
  objective — how far back your last good backup is).

## Ghana in focus

The **CSSPS disruption in 2022** affected around **500,000 students** when a core
placement system went down. Frame it as institutional risk: the *likelihood* of
a targeted attack on a high-visibility national system was real; the *impact* —
half a million families unable to confirm school placements — was severe across
availability, reputation and public trust. Who *owned* that risk before it
happened? Was there an RTO for the service? Those are the questions a risk
register forces you to answer *beforehand*, not during the crisis.

Under the Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038), the CSA can **request your risk
documentation**. A current, owned risk register is not just good practice — it is
something a regulator may ask to see.

## Why it matters

- A ranked risk register tells leadership *exactly* where the next cedi of
  security budget should go.
- Named owners turn a wish list into accountability.
- RTO/RPO agreed in advance prevent the paralysing "how long until we're back?"
  argument during an outage.

## Common mistakes

- **A register with no owners.** It's a wish list, not a risk register.
- **Scoring by gut feel with no evidence.** Tie each score to something real
  (a scan result, an incident, a missing control).
- **Treating all risks equally.** The point is to rank and triage.
- **"Accepting" risk silently.** Every accepted risk needs a name and a date.

## Put it to work

In the course you build an **eight-entry risk register** for a multi-site
institution with cloud records, payment systems and an unpatched remote-access
service: write each risk as an IF/THEN statement, score inherent and residual
risk, assign an owner, map gaps to the six CSF functions, and propose a 90-day
treatment backlog. This feeds directly into the Module 10 capstone and the
Module 9 board memo.

## Key takeaways

- Cyber risk = **likelihood × impact**, expressed as a business consequence.
- Four treatments: **mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept** — accept only with an
  owner + date.
- NIST CSF 2.0 has six functions; **GOVERN** makes it a board duty.
- Set **RTO** (max downtime) and **RPO** (max data loss) per critical service.
- A register without **named owners** is worthless.

## Check yourself

1. **Write this as a risk statement: the payroll server hasn't been patched in a
   year.**
   → *IF an attacker exploits the unpatched payroll server, THEN staff salary
   data could be stolen and we face DPC penalties and payroll disruption.* (Threat
   → vulnerability → business consequence.)

2. **A risk is scored likelihood 2, impact 5. Another is 4×2. Which gets
   attention first, and why isn't it obvious?**
   → Both rate 8–10, so neither dominates on the number alone. Impact-5 (a
   catastrophic but rarer event) often wins because the *consequence* is
   unacceptable even if unlikely — judgement, not just arithmetic.

3. **You buy cyber insurance for a risk. Which treatment is that?**
   → *Transfer* — you shift the financial cost, though the operational pain
   remains, so it's rarely enough on its own.

4. **Which NIST CSF 2.0 function is new, and what does it signal?**
   → *GOVERN* — it signals that cybersecurity accountability sits with the board,
   not just IT.

## Glossary

- **Inherent / residual risk** — risk before / after controls.
- **Risk appetite / tolerance** — how much risk the organisation will accept.
- **RTO / RPO** — max acceptable downtime / data loss per service.
- **NIST CSF 2.0** — the six-function cybersecurity framework (Govern, Identify,
  Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover).
- **Risk owner** — the named person accountable for a specific risk.
