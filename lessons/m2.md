# Module 2 — Institutional Cyber Risk & Digital Resilience

**Day 1 · Afternoon · Leadership module (no Kali lab — you build a risk register)**

Cyber risk is not an IT problem that occasionally bothers the business. It *is*
business risk — operational, financial, legal, safety and reputational, all at
once — that happens to travel through computers. This module gives you the
discipline to measure it, rank it, own it, and decide what to do about it, using
the same frameworks a regulator or an auditor will expect to see.

By the end you will be able to:
- express a technical threat as a board-ready business-risk statement;
- assess risk using a structured method (likelihood × impact, inherent vs
  residual) and know where quantitative methods like FAIR fit;
- place controls against the six NIST CSF 2.0 functions and set RTO/RPO per
  service;
- build a risk register that drives decisions, with named owners and review
  dates.

---

## 1. The big picture: risk is a decision tool, not a feeling

A **risk** is the combination of how *likely* a harmful event is and how *badly*
it would hurt. The purpose of assessing it is singular: **to decide where to
spend finite money and attention first.** You cannot fix everything, so you rank.

Write every risk as a sentence a non-technical director understands:

> IF *[a threat]* exploits *[a vulnerability]*, THEN the institution suffers
> *[a business consequence]*.

*IF an attacker exploits our unpatched internet-facing VPN, THEN citizen records
are stolen and we face DPC penalties, breach-notification costs and loss of
public trust.* Notice the consequence is money, law and trust — not "a box gets
owned". Boards fund consequences they can see.

The vocabulary auditors expect you to use precisely:

- **Threat** (the actor/event), **vulnerability** (the weakness), **likelihood**,
  **impact**.
- **Inherent risk** — the exposure with *no* controls. **Residual risk** — what
  remains *after* your controls. The gap is the value your controls deliver.
- **Risk appetite** — how much risk the organisation is *willing* to take to
  pursue its goals; **risk tolerance** — the acceptable variation around that.
- **Risk owner** — the single named person accountable for a risk.

## 2. How risk assessment actually works

The common method is **qualitative**: score likelihood and impact (say 1–5),
multiply or plot on a 5×5 heat map, and rank. It is fast and communicates well
to a board. Its weakness is false precision — "a 15" feels exact but is a
judgement. Tie every score to **evidence**: a scan result, a past incident, a
missing control, a threat-intel report (Module 1).

Where decisions are large (a multi-million-cedi control, cyber-insurance limits),
professionals add **quantitative** analysis. **FAIR** (Factor Analysis of
Information Risk) decomposes risk into loss-event *frequency* and *magnitude* and
expresses it in money with ranges — e.g. "annual loss exposure: GHS 0.4M–3.1M,
most likely 1.2M". That lets a board compare a security spend against the loss it
avoids, like any other investment. The structured public standards behind all of
this are **NIST SP 800-30** (risk assessment), **ISO/IEC 27005** (risk
management) and **ISO 31000** (enterprise risk).

Four **treatments** — memorise them, because an auditor will ask which you chose
and why:

- **Mitigate** — add controls to cut likelihood or impact (patch, MFA, backups).
- **Transfer** — shift the cost (cyber insurance, a contractual indemnity).
- **Avoid** — stop the risky activity (decommission the exposed service).
- **Accept** — knowingly live with it — **only** with a named owner and a review
  date. *Acceptance without accountability is negligence.*

## 3. NIST CSF 2.0 — the control map boards now answer to

Controls are organised against six functions. CSF 2.0 (2024) added **GOVERN** at
the centre, and that is the headline: cybersecurity is now explicitly a
**board-level accountability**, not a task delegated to "the IT person".

| Function | Plain meaning | Example |
|----------|---------------|---------|
| **GOVERN** | Accountability, strategy, policy, risk appetite | Board approves the cyber-risk policy and budget |
| **IDENTIFY** | Know your assets, data, dependencies and risks | A current asset inventory exists |
| **PROTECT** | Deploy safeguards | MFA everywhere; patching; staff trained |
| **DETECT** | Notice when something's wrong | SIEM alerts reviewed daily |
| **RESPOND** | Act on an incident | A tested plan; who calls whom |
| **RECOVER** | Restore and learn | Backups tested monthly; lessons applied |

Resilience — the "Recover" discipline — is set per critical service by two
numbers business owners agree in advance: **RTO** (Recovery Time Objective — the
longest acceptable downtime) and **RPO** (Recovery Point Objective — the most
data you can afford to lose). These are business decisions, not IT guesses.

## 4. An analogy

Insuring a building against fire: you don't buy a sprinkler for every cupboard on
day one. You ask how likely a fire is and how bad it would be. A paper archive
(high impact) beside an old fuse box (high likelihood) jumps to the top. Then you
choose: sprinklers (**mitigate**), insurance (**transfer**), stop storing paper
there (**avoid**), or decide it's small enough to live with — and write that down
with a name against it (**accept**). Cyber-risk treatment is exactly these four
choices, applied with evidence.

## 5. Scenarios — risk owned, and risk ignored

### Local (Ghana)

- **CSSPS disruption, 2022 (~500,000 students affected).** As institutional risk:
  the *likelihood* of a targeted attack on a high-visibility national system was
  real and knowable; the *impact* — half a million families unable to confirm
  placements — was severe across availability, reputation and public trust. The
  governing questions were owed *before* the event: Who owned this risk? Was
  there an RTO for the service? Was recovery tested? A risk register forces those
  answers in advance.
- Under **Act 1038**, the CSA can request your risk documentation. A current,
  owned register is not just good practice — it is something a regulator may
  demand to see.

### International (well-documented)

- **Equifax, 2017 (147M+ people).** A *known* vulnerability in Apache Struts
  (CVE-2017-5638) had a patch available for months. The risk was identifiable and
  treatable — and effectively *accepted by inaction*, with no owner. This is what
  "acceptance without accountability" looks like at scale.
- **Maersk / NotPetya, 2017 (~US$300M loss).** A destructive worm halted the
  world's largest shipping line. The decisive factor in survival was *resilience*
  — one surviving domain-controller backup in Ghana (by chance) let them rebuild.
  The lesson boards remember: tested recovery (RTO/RPO) is not optional.

The pattern: organisations rarely fail because a risk was *unknown*. They fail
because a known risk had **no owner and no treatment**.

## 6. Why it matters

- A ranked register tells leadership exactly where the next cedi of security
  budget should go — and lets them defend that choice.
- Named owners convert a wish list into accountability.
- RTO/RPO agreed in advance prevent the paralysing "how long until we're back?"
  argument *during* an outage.

## 7. Common mistakes

- **A register with no owners** — a wish list, not a risk register.
- **Scoring by gut** — every score needs evidence behind it.
- **Treating all risks equally** — the entire point is to rank and triage.
- **Silent acceptance** — every accepted risk needs a name and a review date.
- **Confusing appetite with tolerance** — appetite is the target; tolerance is
  the acceptable deviation.

## Key takeaways

- Cyber risk = **likelihood × impact**, written as a **business consequence**.
- **Inherent → residual**; the gap is your controls' value. Use **FAIR** to put
  big decisions in money.
- Four treatments: **mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept** — accept only with owner
  + date.
- **NIST CSF 2.0**'s six functions; **GOVERN** makes cyber a board duty.
- Set **RTO** (downtime) and **RPO** (data loss) per critical service.

## Check yourself

1. **Rewrite as a risk statement: "the payroll server hasn't been patched in a
   year."**
   → *IF an attacker exploits the unpatched payroll server, THEN staff salary
   data is exposed and we face DPC penalties and payroll disruption.*

2. **Risk A scores 2×5, risk B scores 4×2 — both rate ~10. Which gets attention,
   and why isn't the number enough?**
   → Judgement is required: a catastrophic-but-rare impact (A, impact 5) often
   wins because the *consequence* is unacceptable even if unlikely. The score
   ranks; it does not decide.

3. **You buy cyber insurance for a risk. Which treatment, and what does it NOT
   fix?**
   → Transfer — it shifts financial cost but not the operational disruption or
   reputational harm, so it is rarely sufficient alone.

4. **Which NIST CSF 2.0 function is new in 2.0, and what does it signal?**
   → GOVERN — it signals that cybersecurity accountability sits with the board.

5. **Equifax had a patch available for the vulnerability that breached it. In
   risk terms, what went wrong?**
   → A known, treatable risk was effectively *accepted by inaction* — no owner,
   no treatment, no review. That is negligent acceptance.

## Glossary

- **Inherent / residual risk** — exposure before / after controls.
- **Risk appetite / tolerance** — risk the org will take / acceptable variation.
- **FAIR** — quantitative risk model expressing exposure in money.
- **NIST SP 800-30 / ISO 27005 / ISO 31000** — the risk-assessment and
  risk-management standards.
- **NIST CSF 2.0** — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.
- **RTO / RPO** — max acceptable downtime / data loss per service.
- **Risk owner** — the one person accountable for a specific risk.
