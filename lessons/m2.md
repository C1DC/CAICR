## 1 · Risk Is a Decision Tool, Not a Feeling

A **risk** combines how *likely* a harmful event is with how *badly* it would
hurt. The purpose of assessing it is singular: **to decide where to spend finite
money and attention first.** You cannot fix everything, so you rank.

Write every risk as a sentence a non-technical director understands:

> IF *[a threat]* exploits *[a vulnerability]*, THEN the institution suffers
> *[a business consequence]*.

For example: *IF an attacker exploits our unpatched internet-facing VPN, THEN
citizen records are stolen and we face DPC penalties, breach-notification costs
and loss of public trust.* The consequence is money, law and trust — not "a box
gets owned". **Boards fund consequences they can see.**

The vocabulary auditors expect you to use precisely:

- **Threat** (the actor/event) · **vulnerability** (the weakness) · **likelihood** · **impact**.
- **Inherent risk** — exposure with *no* controls. **Residual risk** — what remains *after* controls. The gap is the value your controls deliver.
- **Risk appetite** — how much risk the organisation is *willing* to take to pursue its goals. **Risk tolerance** — the acceptable variation around that appetite for a specific risk.
- **Risk owner** — the single named person accountable for a risk.

::: key | Name the property first
Before scoring anything, state which of Module 1's five security properties the
risk threatens (confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity,
continuity). It focuses the whole assessment — a confidentiality risk and an
availability risk need different controls and different consequences.
:::

## 2 · How Risk Assessment Actually Works

The common method is **qualitative**: score likelihood and impact (say 1–5),
multiply or plot on a 5×5 heat map, and rank. It is fast and communicates to a
board. Its weakness is false precision — "a 15" feels exact but is a judgement.
Tie every score to **evidence**: a scan result, a past incident, a missing
control, a threat-intel report (Module 1).

| | **1 Negligible** | **2 Minor** | **3 Moderate** | **4 Major** | **5 Severe** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5 Almost certain** | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
| **4 Likely** | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| **3 Possible** | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 15 |
| **2 Unlikely** | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
| **1 Rare** | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |

Where decisions are large (a multi-million-cedi control, cyber-insurance limits),
professionals add **quantitative** analysis. **FAIR** (Factor Analysis of
Information Risk) decomposes risk into loss-event *frequency* and *magnitude* and
expresses it in money with ranges — e.g. "annual loss exposure: GHS 0.4M–3.1M,
most likely 1.2M". That lets a board compare a security spend against the loss it
avoids, like any other investment. The public standards behind all of this are
**NIST SP 800-30** (risk assessment), **ISO/IEC 27005** (information-risk
management) and **ISO 31000** (enterprise risk).

::: note | Qualitative vs quantitative — when to use which
Use the **5×5 heat map** to triage the whole register quickly and communicate to
the board. Reach for **FAIR** on the two or three biggest decisions, where
turning risk into a cedi range justifies (or kills) a large spend.
:::

money by decomposing it into two things you *can* estimate, then multiplying:

**Annual Loss Exposure (ALE) = Loss Event Frequency (LEF) × Loss Magnitude (LM).**

**Worked example — Sankofa Rural Bank's BEC fraud risk.** The team estimates,
from the near-miss and peer data, that a *successful* fraudulent payment would
happen about **twice a year** (LEF = 2). Each event averages **GHS 600,000** of
diverted funds plus **GHS 300,000** of investigation, recovery and regulatory
cost — **LM ≈ GHS 900,000**. So:

> **ALE = 2 × GHS 900,000 = GHS 1,800,000 per year** (most likely), modelled as a
> *range* — say GHS 0.4M (one small event) to GHS 6M (a bad year) — because every
> input is an estimate, not a fact.

Now the board can compare: a **GHS 150,000** dual-control + callback programme
against a **GHS 1.8M/year** exposure is an obvious yes. That is the power of
quantification — it converts "that feels risky" into an investment decision. Note
FAIR never claims false precision: it always carries a **range**, and you state
your confidence.

## 3 · The Four Treatments

Memorise them, because an auditor will ask which you chose and why:

- **Mitigate** — add controls to cut likelihood or impact (patch, MFA, backups, segmentation).
- **Transfer** — shift the cost (cyber insurance, a contractual indemnity). Note: it shifts *financial* cost, not the operational disruption or the reputational harm.
- **Avoid** — stop the risky activity (decommission the exposed service, end the data collection).
- **Accept** — knowingly live with it — **only** with a named owner and a review date.

::: warning | Acceptance without accountability is negligence
Every accepted risk needs a **named owner** and a **review date**, recorded in the
register. "We decided to accept it" with no name and no date is not a decision —
it is an unmanaged exposure that a regulator will treat as negligence.
:::

| Function | Plain meaning | Example for a Ghanaian institution |
|----------|---------------|-----------------------------------|
| **GOVERN** | Accountability, strategy, policy, risk appetite | Board approves the cyber-risk policy and budget; CISO has authority |
| **IDENTIFY** | Know your assets, data, dependencies and risks | A current asset inventory exists; crown-jewel data documented |
| **PROTECT** | Deploy safeguards | MFA on all accounts; patching; email filtering; staff trained |
| **DETECT** | Notice when something's wrong | SIEM running; alerts reviewed daily |
| **RESPOND** | Act on an incident | A tested plan; the team knows who calls whom |
| **RECOVER** | Restore and improve | Backups tested monthly; lessons from incidents applied |

A **current/target profile** rates the institution against each function today
(0–4 maturity) and sets a realistic target — the gap list becomes your plan.

## 5 · Resilience — RTO, RPO and the Link to Continuity

Risk treatment reduces the chance and cost of harm; **resilience** is the ability
to keep running and recover when harm lands anyway. Two numbers, agreed with the
business per critical service, drive it:

- **RTO** (Recovery Time Objective) — the maximum acceptable *downtime* before
  real harm. Sometimes expressed as **MTD** (Maximum Tolerable Downtime).
- **RPO** (Recovery Point Objective) — the maximum acceptable *data loss*, i.e.
  how far back your last good backup is. Sometimes expressed as **MADL** (Maximum
  Acceptable Data Loss).

These are **business decisions**, not IT guesses, and they feed the Business
Impact Analysis and recovery priorities you build in Module 9.

## 6 · Scenarios — Risk Owned, and Risk Ignored

::: ghana | CSSPS disruption, 2022 (~500,000 students)
As institutional risk: the *likelihood* of a targeted attack on a
high-visibility national system was real and knowable; the *impact* — half a
million families unable to confirm placements — was severe across availability,
reputation and public trust. The governing questions were owed *before* the
event: Who owned this risk? Was there an RTO for the service? Was recovery
tested? A risk register forces those answers in advance. Under **Act 1038** the
CSA can request your risk documentation — a current, owned register is something
a regulator may demand to see.
:::

::: case | Equifax, 2017 (147M+ people) — negligent acceptance
A *known* vulnerability in Apache Struts (CVE-2017-5638) had a patch available
for months. The risk was identifiable and treatable — and effectively *accepted
by inaction*, with no owner. This is what "acceptance without accountability"
looks like at scale.
:::

::: case | Maersk / NotPetya, 2017 (~US$300M) — resilience saved them
A destructive worm halted the world's largest shipping line. Survival hinged on
a single surviving domain-controller backup (offline, by chance, in a Ghana
office). The lesson boards now cite: **tested recovery (RTO/RPO) is not
optional**, and luck is not a strategy.
:::

The pattern: organisations rarely fail because a risk was *unknown*. They fail
because a known risk had **no owner and no treatment**.

## 7 · Running the Assessment in Practice

A risk assessment is not a spreadsheet filled in alone at a desk — it is a
**facilitated workshop** that turns scattered knowledge into ranked decisions.

**Who is in the room.** The risk owner/facilitator (you), someone who knows the
*business* (operations, finance), someone who knows the *systems* (IT), and —
for the sign-off — someone with *authority to accept risk* (an executive). Cyber
risk lives at the intersection of all three; none of them can do it alone.

**The inputs you bring.** Your Module 1 threat intelligence (who would attack and
how), an **asset inventory** (what matters, and how critical), **incident history
and near-misses** (evidence for *likelihood*), and any **external assessment** —
a pen-test or audit (evidence for *vulnerability*). Opinions without these are
guesses.

**The five steps, in the room:**

1. **Identify** — list the risks as IF/THEN business statements; name the security
   property each threatens.
2. **Score inherent** — L × I with *no* controls, each score tied to a piece of
   evidence you just listed ("3 staff fell for the last phish" → likelihood is not
   a 1).
3. **Credit controls → residual** — only count a control if it genuinely lowers L
   or I. If the residual equals the inherent, the "control" is theatre.
4. **Treat & assign** — pick mitigate / transfer / avoid / accept, name a single
   **owner**, set a **review date**. Quantify the top two or three with **FAIR**.
5. **Rank & schedule** — sort by **residual** rating; the top items become the
   **90-day treatment backlog** leadership funds.

### 7.1 Communicating it to the board

A board does not read a 40-row register. It acts on a **risk-on-a-page**:

- the **top 5 risks by residual**, each as one business sentence + the decision asked;
- the **two or three FAIR numbers** (exposure in GHS vs the cost to reduce it);
- the **heat map** so directors see the shape at a glance;
- a short list of **accepted** risks (each with its owner) — so acceptance is a
  conscious board act, not a silent omission.

Directors will ask three things, so have the answers ready: *"What is our biggest
exposure in cedis?"*, *"Who owns it?"*, and *"What do you need from us?"* If your
register can't answer those in one page, it isn't finished.

### 7.2 The register itself (your deliverable)

Every row carries: the **IF/THEN statement**, an **inherent** score (L×I), the
**controls** in place or planned, a **residual** score, a **treatment**, a named
**owner**, and a **review date**. Top risks by residual are acted on first; gaps
map to the six CSF functions; actions become the 90-day backlog.

::: note | Worked example (one row)
*IF an attacker exploits our unpatched remote-access service (no MFA), THEN
citizen records are exfiltrated and we face DPC penalties and public-trust loss.*
— Inherent **4×5 = 20**; controls: patch SLA + MFA + monitoring; Residual **2×5 =
10**; Treatment **Mitigate**; Owner **Head of IT**; Review **2026-01-15**. The
full eight-entry model is in `labctl worksheet m2`.
:::

## Key takeaways

- Cyber risk = **likelihood × impact**, written as a **business consequence**.
- **Inherent → residual**; the gap is your controls' value. Use **FAIR** to put big decisions in money.
- Four treatments: **mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept** — accept only with an owner + date.
- **NIST CSF 2.0**'s six functions; **GOVERN** makes cyber a board duty.
- Set **RTO** (downtime) and **RPO** (data loss) per critical service.
- A register without **named owners** is a wish list.

## Check yourself

1. **Rewrite as a risk statement: "the payroll server hasn't been patched in a year."**
   → *IF an attacker exploits the unpatched payroll server, THEN staff salary data is exposed and we face DPC penalties and payroll disruption.*

2. **Risk A scores 2×5, risk B scores 4×2 — both ≈10. Which gets attention, and why isn't the number enough?**
   → Judgement: a catastrophic-but-rare impact (A, impact 5) often wins because the *consequence* is unacceptable even if unlikely. The score ranks; it does not decide.

3. **You buy cyber insurance for a risk — which treatment, and what does it NOT fix?**
   → Transfer — it shifts financial cost but not the operational disruption or reputational harm, so it is rarely sufficient alone.

4. **Which NIST CSF 2.0 function is new in 2.0, and what does it signal?**
   → GOVERN — cybersecurity accountability sits with the board.

5. **Equifax had a patch available for the vulnerability that breached it. In risk terms, what went wrong?**
   → A known, treatable risk was effectively *accepted by inaction* — no owner, no treatment, no review. Negligent acceptance.

## Glossary

- **Inherent / residual risk** — exposure before / after controls.
- **Risk appetite / tolerance** — risk the organisation will take / acceptable variation on a specific risk.
- **FAIR** — quantitative model expressing exposure in money.
- **NIST SP 800-30 / ISO 27005 / ISO 31000** — risk-assessment / information-risk / enterprise-risk standards.
- **NIST CSF 2.0** — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.
- **RTO / RPO (MTD / MADL)** — max downtime / max data loss per service.
- **Risk owner** — the one person accountable for a specific risk.
