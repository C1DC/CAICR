# Module 1 — Threat Landscape & Cyber Threat Intelligence

**Day 1 · Morning · Hands-on lab: `labctl start m1`**

Before you defend an organisation you have to answer three questions the way an
intelligence analyst does: **who** would attack it, **why**, and **how** — and
then you learn to gather that understanding first-hand, using the same public
sources and tools an attacker uses against you. This module takes you from the
theory of threat intelligence to a terminal running real reconnaissance, in one
morning.

By the end you will be able to:
- place any attack on the Cyber Kill Chain and map its behaviour to MITRE ATT&CK;
- distinguish the four levels of threat intelligence and write to the right one;
- grade a source and a claim, and separate fact from assessment with a stated
  confidence level;
- run passive DNS and OSINT reconnaissance, read email-authentication records,
  and turn findings into a defensible threat brief.

---

## 1. The big picture: intelligence, not alarmism

Most organisations react to cyber threats emotionally — a headline appears, a
panic follows, money is spent on the wrong thing. **Cyber threat intelligence
(CTI)** replaces that with discipline: evidence-led understanding of adversaries,
expressed with confidence levels, aimed at a decision.

Good CTI has three properties. It is **actionable** (it changes a decision),
**timely** (it arrives while the decision is still open), and **accurate** (it
separates what is known from what is believed). An intelligence product that
does not change what someone does is trivia, however interesting.

Everything you protect reduces to five security properties — **Confidentiality,
Integrity, Availability, Authenticity** and **business Continuity**. When an
incident lands, the first analytic move is to name which of the five just broke:
ransomware breaks *availability*; a leaked database breaks *confidentiality*; a
forged payment instruction breaks *integrity* and *authenticity* at once. Naming
the property focuses the response.

## 2. Know your adversary: the threat-actor taxonomy

Attacks come from people with motives, resources and patience that differ wildly.
Defending a rural clinic and defending the Bank of Ghana are different problems
because the adversaries are different.

| Actor | Motivation | Typical resourcing | What they do |
|-------|-----------|--------------------|--------------|
| Organised cybercrime | Money | High, professionalised | Ransomware, BEC, fraud, data theft-for-sale |
| Nation-state / APT | Espionage, disruption, strategic advantage | Very high, patient (months–years) | Long-dwell intrusions, supply-chain, sabotage |
| Hacktivists | Ideology, publicity | Low–medium | Defacement, data leaks, DDoS |
| Insiders | Grievance, greed, or simple carelessness | Native access already | Data exfiltration, sabotage, accidental exposure |
| Opportunists ("script kiddies") | Curiosity, notoriety | Low, using others' tools | Mass-scan the internet and hit whatever is exposed |

Two sobering truths. First, **organised crime is the dominant threat to West
African institutions** — the money is in fraud and ransomware, not espionage.
Second, you become a target simply by being reachable: opportunists scan the
entire IPv4 internet continuously, so an exposed service in Accra is found in
hours, by someone who never heard of you and does not care who you are.

## 3. The frameworks professionals actually use

Analysts do not improvise vocabulary. Four models give the whole field a shared,
precise language. Learn them; you will use them all week.

**The Cyber Kill Chain (Lockheed Martin)** describes an intrusion as seven
stages: *Reconnaissance → Weaponization → Delivery → Exploitation → Installation
→ Command & Control → Actions on Objectives.* Its value is defensive: the
earlier in the chain you detect and break it, the cheaper the incident.
Reconnaissance — this module — is stage one, and the only stage that happens
entirely outside your walls.

**MITRE ATT&CK** is the modern, granular catalogue of real-world adversary
behaviour, organised as *tactics* (the goal, e.g. `TA0043 Reconnaissance`) and
*techniques* (the method, e.g. `T1590 Gather Victim Network Information`). When
you write "the actor used `T1566` (Phishing) for initial access", any defender
on earth knows exactly what you mean. ATT&CK is the lingua franca of CTI.

**The Diamond Model** frames every intrusion event as four linked vertices —
**Adversary, Capability, Infrastructure, Victim**. It is how you pivot: from a
malicious *infrastructure* (a domain, an IP) you reason toward the *adversary*
and their other *capabilities*.

**The Pyramid of Pain (David Bianco)** ranks indicators by how much it *hurts the
attacker* when you block them. From easiest-to-change (least pain) to hardest:
*hash values → IP addresses → domain names → host/network artefacts → tools →
TTPs.* Blocking a file hash is trivial for the attacker to evade (recompile);
forcing them to abandon their *techniques* is agony. Good intelligence pushes
your defence up the pyramid — away from brittle indicators, toward behaviour.

## 4. IOCs vs IOAs, and the four levels of intelligence

An **Indicator of Compromise (IOC)** is forensic evidence that something *has
happened* — a known-bad IP, a file hash, a C2 domain. An **Indicator of Attack
(IOA)** describes behaviour *as it unfolds* — "a process spawned PowerShell which
opened a network connection". IOCs are backward-looking and brittle (bottom of
the pyramid); IOAs are behavioural and durable (top). Mature detection leans on
IOAs.

Intelligence is also written for four different audiences, and the commonest
failure is pitching it to the wrong one:

- **Strategic** — for the board: trends, risk, business impact. No jargon.
- **Operational** — for security leadership: who is targeting our sector, and how.
- **Tactical** — for defenders: the TTPs to hunt and the controls to deploy.
- **Technical** — for the SOC/tooling: the specific IOCs to block.

## 5. Grade your sources and your claims — never state a hypothesis as fact

Professionals attach confidence to every claim. A lightweight version of the
**Admiralty (NATO) system** grades the *source reliability* (A reliable … F
cannot be judged) and the *information credibility* (1 confirmed … 6 cannot be
judged) separately — because a reliable source can still pass on a rumour.
In practice you will at minimum tag each finding **High / Medium / Low**
confidence and keep **facts** ("`dig` returned no DMARC record") strictly apart
from **assessments** ("we assess spoofing is likely"). The discipline of writing
*"we assess X (medium confidence) because Y"* is what makes you trustworthy to a
decision-maker — and defensible afterwards.

Intelligence is shared in standard formats — **STIX** (the data model) over
**TAXII** (the transport) — so machines can exchange IOCs. In Ghana, the national
coordination point is **CERT-GH**, under the **Cyber Security Authority (CSA)**,
which publishes advisories at `csa.gov.gh`. Reading them is itself CTI: it tells
you what is hitting Ghanaian organisations *right now*.

## 6. How reconnaissance actually works

Recon splits in two. **Passive** reconnaissance touches only *public* sources —
DNS, WHOIS, certificate-transparency logs, search engines, social media, breach
dumps — so the target cannot tell you looked. **Active** reconnaissance probes
the target's own systems (port scans); it is louder and, without authorisation,
illegal. This module is strictly passive. Module 3 is where you go active, and
only against the lab range.

Three tools carry the lab:

**`dig`** queries DNS — the internet's address book — and reveals a domain's mail
servers (`MX`), its published anti-spoofing policy (`TXT` records for SPF and
DMARC), its name servers and its addresses.

```
dig example.com A +short          # the IPv4 address(es)
dig example.com MX +short         # who handles its mail
dig example.com TXT +short        # SPF lives here (v=spf1 ...)
dig _dmarc.example.com TXT +short # DMARC policy lives here
```

**`whois`** shows who registered a domain and *when*. A look-alike domain
registered three days before a fraudulent invoice is one of the strongest signals
in all of fraud investigation.

**`theHarvester`** sweeps public sources for a domain's email addresses and host
names — the raw material an attacker uses to build a phishing target list. The
same output tells a defender exactly who needs the most awareness training.

### Reading email authentication — the finding that matters most

Three DNS-published controls decide whether anyone can forge mail from a domain.
Understand them properly; most phishing and all domain-spoofing BEC turns on them.

- **SPF** (Sender Policy Framework) lists which servers may send for the domain,
  as a TXT record ending in a qualifier. `-all` is a **hard fail** (reject
  everything else); `~all` is a **soft fail** ("probably forged, but deliver
  anyway"); `?all` is neutral and worthless. Example:
  `v=spf1 ip4:41.210.15.21 include:_spf.google.com -all`.
- **DKIM** (DomainKeys Identified Mail) attaches a cryptographic signature,
  published at a *selector* (`selector._domainkey.example.com`), proving the
  message was not altered and genuinely came from the domain.
- **DMARC** ties them together and sets the **policy**, published at
  `_dmarc.example.com`: `v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com`. The
  policy `p` is the whole game — `none` (monitor only, no protection),
  `quarantine` (send to spam), or `reject` (refuse outright). Crucially, DMARC
  also requires **alignment**: the SPF/DKIM-validated domain must match the
  visible `From:` domain, which is what actually stops display-name spoofing.

**No DMARC record, or `p=none`, means the domain is effectively spoofable** — an
attacker can send mail that appears to come from it and inboxes will accept it.
Publishing `p=reject` is free and is the single highest-value anti-phishing
control an organisation can deploy for its own brand.

Finally, map what you find to ATT&CK: DNS/network recon is **T1590** (*Gather
Victim Network Information*); harvesting staff emails is **T1589** (*Gather Victim
Identity Information*); probing by sending bait is **T1598** (*Phishing for
Information*).

## 7. Scenarios — how this plays out in the real world

### Local (Ghana)

- **GCB Bank BEC (~GHS 2.3 million).** The attack *started* as pure Module-1
  reconnaissance: the criminals profiled the procurement team, registered a
  look-alike supplier domain weeks ahead, and studied the relationship before a
  single email was sent. Had anyone run `dig`/`whois` against their supplier
  domains, the missing DMARC and the freshly-registered look-alike would have
  stood out before the money moved.
- **CSSPS disruption, 2022** — a targeted attack on a national school-placement
  system affected roughly half a million students. A high-visibility public
  system is a high-value target; reconnaissance of such systems is constant.
- **SIM-swap / MoMo fraud wave, 2022–2023** — systematic recon of victims
  (numbers, names, routines) fed social-engineering of the mobile operators.
- **GRA-themed phishing** — mass credential-harvesting that begins with
  harvesting taxpayer email addresses — textbook `T1589`.

### International (well-documented)

- **Bangladesh Bank heist, 2016 (~US$81 million).** Attackers linked to the
  Lazarus Group spent *months* on reconnaissance inside the bank's network before
  issuing fraudulent SWIFT transfers. The theft was the last act of a long,
  patient recon-and-intrusion campaign — the Kill Chain in full.
- **Colonial Pipeline, 2021.** Ransomware (DarkSide) that shut fuel supply to the
  US east coast began with a *single* exposed, reused VPN credential with no MFA —
  a reconnaissance-and-credentials failure, not an exotic exploit.
- **Twitter, July 2020.** Attackers social-engineered employees (vishing) after
  reconnaissance of staff and internal tools, then hijacked high-profile
  accounts. Human-layer recon, not a software bug.
- **Business Email Compromise globally** — the FBI's IC3 attributes well over
  US$50 billion in exposed losses to BEC between 2013 and 2023, making it one of
  the costliest categories of cybercrime on earth. Almost every case opens with
  the reconnaissance you practise here.

The through-line: **serious attacks begin with reconnaissance**, and most of that
reconnaissance is passive and legal — which means a defender can run exactly the
same checks and see exactly what the attacker sees, first.

## 8. Writing the threat brief

Findings are worthless until a decision-maker can act on them. Structure a brief
**BLUF-first** (Bottom Line Up Front): the key judgement and the recommended
action in the first two sentences. Then the verified indicators (facts, with
sources), then the assessment (hypotheses, each with a confidence level), then
recommended actions, and finally an honest statement of what you do *not* yet
know. Write to the audience level (§4). Never bury the decision at the bottom,
and never present an assessment as a fact.

## Ghana in focus

CERT-GH advisories (`csa.gov.gh`) are the single best free intelligence source
for the Ghanaian threat picture — they translate the global trends above into
"what is targeting banks, telcos and ministries here, this month". Building the
habit of reading them, and of running passive checks on your *own* domains before
an attacker does, is the practical heart of this module.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating `~all` as protection.** A soft-fail SPF still delivers forged mail;
  without DMARC `p=reject`, the domain is spoofable.
- **Confusing SPF with DMARC.** SPF authorises senders; only DMARC sets an
  enforcement policy and demands alignment. You need all three (SPF, DKIM, DMARC).
- **Stating hypotheses as facts.** Every assessment carries a confidence level.
- **Chasing brittle indicators.** Blocking one hash or IP is bottom-of-pyramid;
  durable defence targets TTPs.
- **Running active tools during "recon".** Passive means passive — touching the
  target's systems can itself be an offence.

## Do it yourself

`labctl start m1` gives you an isolated DNS server for a fictional organisation
and a captured OSINT report. Use `dig` to pull its MX, SPF and DMARC records,
notice the weak `~all` and the *absent* DMARC, read the OSINT report for exposed
staff, map it to ATT&CK, and write five verified indicators into a one-page
threat brief. `labctl verify m1` grades your findings.

## Key takeaways

- CTI is **actionable, timely, accurate** intelligence — discipline, not panic.
- Speak in frameworks: **Kill Chain** (where in the intrusion), **ATT&CK** (which
  technique), **Diamond** (how to pivot), **Pyramid of Pain** (how durable the
  indicator).
- **IOC = what happened; IOA = behaviour now.** Push defence up the pyramid.
- Write to the right **level** (strategic/operational/tactical/technical) and tag
  **confidence**; keep fact separate from assessment.
- **No DMARC / `p=none` = spoofable.** SPF `-all` + DKIM + DMARC `p=reject` is the
  goal. Recon techniques: **T1590 / T1589 / T1598.**

## Check yourself

1. **A domain publishes `v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all` and has no
   `_dmarc` record. How exposed is it to spoofing, and why?**
   → Highly exposed. `~all` only soft-fails forged mail (still delivered), and
   with no DMARC there is no policy to reject it and no alignment check — an
   attacker can send mail that appears to come from the domain and it will land.

2. **You can block an attacker's file hash, their C2 IP, or force them to change
   their techniques. Rank these by the Pyramid of Pain.**
   → Techniques (most pain — hardest to change) > C2 IP > file hash (least pain —
   trivially recompiled/rotated). Aim your defence as high as you can.

3. **An IOC vs an IOA — give one of each for a brute-force login.**
   → IOC: a known-bad source IP seen in the logs (backward-looking). IOA: "many
   failed authentications from one source followed by a success" (behaviour as it
   happens).

4. **Which is passive reconnaissance: `nmap -sV target`, or `dig target MX`? Why
   does the distinction matter legally?**
   → `dig` (it only reads public DNS; it never touches the target's systems).
   `nmap -sV` actively probes the target — active recon, which without written
   authorisation is an offence under Act 1038.

5. **You brief the board that "we assess, with medium confidence, that our
   supplier domain is being prepared for a spoofing campaign." What two things
   make this a professional statement rather than alarmism?**
   → It is labelled an *assessment* (not a fact) and it carries an explicit
   *confidence level* — and it would be followed by the facts and recommended
   action behind it.

## Glossary

- **CTI** — cyber threat intelligence: evidence-led understanding of adversaries.
- **Cyber Kill Chain** — the 7-stage model of an intrusion (recon → actions).
- **MITRE ATT&CK** — catalogue of adversary tactics (`TA…`) and techniques (`T…`).
- **Diamond Model** — adversary · capability · infrastructure · victim.
- **Pyramid of Pain** — indicators ranked by how much blocking them hurts the
  attacker.
- **IOC / IOA** — indicator of compromise (what happened) / of attack (behaviour).
- **SPF / DKIM / DMARC** — authorised-sender list / signature / enforcement policy
  with alignment.
- **STIX / TAXII** — the standard data model / transport for sharing intelligence.
- **Admiralty grading** — grading source reliability and information credibility
  separately.
- **CERT-GH / CSA** — Ghana's national incident-response team / Cyber Security
  Authority.
