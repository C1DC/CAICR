# Module 1 — Threat Landscape & Cyber Threat Intelligence

**Day 1 · Morning · Hands-on lab: `labctl start m1`**

In one sentence: before you can defend an organisation, you have to understand
*who* would attack it, *why*, and *how* — and then learn to gather that
understanding yourself, the same way the attackers gather it about you.

By the end of this module you will be able to:
- explain the five security properties and the main types of threat actor in
  plain language;
- run passive reconnaissance against a domain and read what it tells an attacker;
- spot a missing email-authentication control (DMARC) that makes spoofing easy;
- turn raw findings into a short, honest threat brief for a decision-maker.

---

## The big picture

Every security decision you will ever make protects one of five things:

- **Confidentiality** — only the right people can see the data.
- **Integrity** — the data has not been secretly changed.
- **Availability** — the system is there when people need it.
- **Authenticity** — the person or system is really who they claim to be.
- **Business continuity** — the organisation keeps functioning through trouble.

The first three (the classic **CIA triad**) are the core; the last two matter
enormously in the real world, especially for banks, hospitals and government.
A useful habit: when something goes wrong, ask *"which of these five just
broke?"* A ransomware attack breaks **availability**. A leaked customer
database breaks **confidentiality**. A forged payment instruction breaks
**integrity** and **authenticity** at once.

Attacks don't come from a faceless "hacker in a hoodie". They come from people
with motives. The main **threat actors** are:

- **Organised criminals** — in it for money. Ransomware, fraud, BEC. This is
  the dominant threat to Ghanaian institutions.
- **Insiders** — staff or contractors, malicious or just careless. The
  over-privileged employee who clicks a link is as dangerous as the angry one.
- **Nation-state actors** — well-funded, patient, after espionage or disruption.
- **Hacktivists** — want attention for a cause; typically defacement or leaks.
- **Opportunists / "script kiddies"** — use ready-made tools against whoever is
  exposed. You become a target simply by having an open door.

Knowing *who* is likely to target you changes *how* you defend. A rural clinic
and the Bank of Ghana face different adversaries and need different priorities.

## An analogy

Think of a burglar casing a house before breaking in. They don't smash a window
on day one. First they walk past, note which lights are on, read the name on the
post box, check the bins for delivery labels, watch when the family leaves.
*None of that is illegal and none of it touches the house.* By the time they
act, they already know the easy way in.

**Reconnaissance** is exactly that, for networks. Attackers read your public DNS
records, your staff email addresses on LinkedIn, your expired certificates —
all public, all "passive". Cyber threat intelligence is you learning to walk
past your own house and see what the burglar sees.

## How it actually works

Reconnaissance splits into two kinds:

1. **Passive** — you only touch *public* sources, never the target's own
   systems. DNS lookups, WHOIS registration data, search engines, breach dumps,
   social media. The target cannot tell you were looking. This is where M1 lives.
2. **Active** — you probe the target directly (port scans, etc.). Louder, and in
   many places illegal without permission. That's Module 3.

The three passive tools you'll use:

- **`dig`** queries DNS — the internet's address book. It reveals a domain's
  mail servers (`MX`), its published anti-spoofing policy (`TXT` records for
  **SPF** and **DMARC**), its name servers, and its addresses.
- **`whois`** shows who registered a domain and *when*. A look-alike domain
  registered three days before a fraud email is a screaming red flag.
- **`theHarvester`** sweeps public sources for a domain's email addresses and
  host names — the raw material for a phishing target list.

The single most important finding in this module is about **email spoofing**.
Three DNS-based controls decide whether an attacker can forge mail from your
domain:

- **SPF** says *which servers are allowed to send mail* for your domain. It ends
  in a qualifier: `-all` (hard fail — reject everything else) or the weaker
  `~all` (soft fail — "probably bad, but deliver anyway").
- **DKIM** adds a cryptographic signature proving the message wasn't altered.
- **DMARC** ties them together and tells receivers *what to do* when SPF/DKIM
  fail — and whether to report abuse back to you. **No DMARC record means no
  policy: anyone can spoof your domain and mail still lands in inboxes.**

Once you have findings, you **map them to MITRE ATT&CK** — a shared catalogue of
attacker techniques. Reconnaissance of this kind is tactic **TA0043**, technique
**T1590** (*Gather Victim Network Information*). Speaking in ATT&CK IDs lets
defenders worldwide compare notes precisely instead of waving their hands.

## Ghana in focus

In the **GCB Bank BEC** case, roughly **GHS 2.3 million** was lost after
attackers impersonated a supplier by email. The attack *started* with
reconnaissance exactly like M1: the criminals identified the finance team,
registered a look-alike supplier domain weeks ahead, and studied the
relationship — all before a single malicious email was sent. Had anyone on the
defending side run the same passive checks on their own supplier domains, the
missing DMARC and the freshly-registered look-alike would have stood out.

CERT-GH (the national incident response team, under the Cyber Security Authority)
publishes advisories at **csa.gov.gh**. Reading them is itself a form of threat
intelligence — it tells you what is actively hitting Ghanaian organisations
*right now*.

## Why it matters

Reconnaissance findings are cheap to fix and devastating to ignore:

- Publishing a strong **DMARC** record (`p=reject`) is free and blocks the most
  common phishing technique against your brand.
- Knowing which staff emails are public tells you who needs the most phishing
  training.
- A threat brief that separates **fact from assumption** lets leaders act
  without panicking. "We confirmed the supplier domain has no DMARC (fact); we
  assess spoofing is likely (assessment, medium confidence)" is useful. "We got
  hacked!" is not.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating `~all` as safe.** A soft-fail SPF still delivers forged mail.
  Weak SPF with no DMARC is barely better than nothing.
- **Confusing SPF and DMARC.** SPF alone, without DMARC, has no enforcement
  policy. Both are needed.
- **Stating assumptions as facts.** Intelligence always carries a *confidence
  level*. Never present a hypothesis as proven.
- **Running active tools in the recon phase.** Passive means passive — touching
  the target's systems during "recon" can itself be an offence.

## Key takeaways

- Five properties: **confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity,
  continuity** — name the one that broke.
- Attackers are **people with motives**; organised crime is Ghana's top threat.
- **Passive recon** reads public data without touching the target — DNS, WHOIS,
  OSINT.
- **No DMARC = spoofable.** `~all` SPF is weak; `-all` + DMARC `p=reject` is the
  goal.
- Map findings to **MITRE ATT&CK** and write briefs that separate **fact from
  assessment**.

## Check yourself

1. **A hospital's records system is knocked offline during surgery. Which
   security property failed?**
   → *Availability.* The data wasn't stolen or changed; it simply wasn't there
   when needed — which in healthcare can be the most dangerous failure of all.

2. **You find a domain publishes SPF ending in `~all` and has no `_dmarc`
   record. Why is that dangerous?**
   → `~all` tells receivers forged mail is only "probably" bad, and the absence
   of DMARC means there is no instruction to reject it. An attacker can spoof the
   domain and the mail still reaches inboxes.

3. **Which is passive reconnaissance?**
   (a) Running Nmap against the target's servers
   (b) Querying the target's DNS records with `dig`
   → *(b).* `dig` reads the public DNS address book; it never touches the
   target's own systems. Nmap actively probes them — that's active recon.

4. **What does mapping a finding to MITRE ATT&CK `T1590` give you?**
   → A precise, shared label ("Gather Victim Network Information") so any
   defender anywhere knows exactly which attacker behaviour you mean.

## Glossary

- **OSINT** — open-source intelligence; information gathered from public sources.
- **SPF / DKIM / DMARC** — the three DNS-based email-authentication controls.
- **IOC** — indicator of compromise (e.g., a malicious IP); evidence an attack
  happened. Contrast **IOA**, indicator of attack (behaviour in progress).
- **MITRE ATT&CK** — a public catalogue of real-world attacker tactics and
  techniques, each with an ID like `T1590`.
- **Confidence level** — how sure you are of an intelligence claim; always state
  it.
