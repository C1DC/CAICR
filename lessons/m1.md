# Module 1 — Threat Landscape & Cyber Threat Intelligence

**Day 1 · Morning Session · ~4.5 hours · Hands-on lab: `labctl start m1`**

This module is the intellectual foundation on which the entire five-day
programme rests. Everything that follows — network defence, vulnerability
management, incident response, digital forensics — depends on an analyst's
ability to think clearly about adversaries: who they are, how they operate, what
intelligence about them looks like, and how that intelligence should shape
decisions. A defender who cannot characterise a threat cannot prioritise
controls, cannot write a credible risk register, and cannot brief a board.

The module operates across two registers simultaneously. The first is
**conceptual**: the frameworks, vocabularies and analytic disciplines that
distinguish professional threat intelligence from alarmist headlines. The second
is **practical**: the terminal commands and investigative workflows that let you
gather and verify intelligence about an organisation in real time. Both are
treated with equal rigour.

8. Map reconnaissance findings to MITRE ATT&CK Reconnaissance tactics (TA0043).

10. Situate the Ghana threat landscape within global trends and cite obligations under the Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038).

## 1 · The Intelligence Problem

### 1.1 Why intelligence, not information

Organisations are drowning in security data. A mid-sized Ghanaian bank with a
modern SIEM generates tens of thousands of log events a day. A ministry receives
dozens of phishing emails a week. A fintech sees thousands of failed logins a
month. The raw quantity is not the problem — the problem is that most of it is
noise, and without a disciplined analytic process the signal is lost.

Intelligence is **not data** and **not information**. It is the product of a
deliberate process that transforms raw observations into *actionable judgements
about adversaries*. The distinction has operational consequences: an
organisation that mistakes "we have a lot of data" for "we have intelligence"
spends money on the wrong controls, responds to the wrong incidents, and briefs
the board on the wrong risks.

The **intelligence cycle** — the model used by national agencies worldwide and
adapted for cyber — has five phases:

1. **Planning & Direction** — what questions need answering? Who needs the answer, by when?
2. **Collection** — what sources exist? Which are reliable? What can be gathered without authorisation?
3. **Processing** — raw data is normalised, translated, made human- or machine-readable.
4. **Analysis & Production** — processed data is evaluated, correlated, and turned into judgements with confidence levels.
5. **Dissemination** — the finished product reaches the right audience, in the right format, at the right time.

The cycle is iterative: dissemination triggers new direction, which restarts
collection. A team that runs it well is not reacting to incidents — it is
anticipating them.

::: quote | 
"Intelligence is the ability to reduce uncertainty about an adversary's intent
and capability to a level at which decision-makers can act with confidence. It
is not the elimination of uncertainty — that is impossible — but its disciplined
management." — adapted from U.S. Intelligence Community Directive 203
:::

### 1.2 The three properties of good CTI

Useful cyber threat intelligence has three properties. When any one is absent,
the product fails its purpose.

**Actionability.** Intelligence that does not change a decision is trivia,
however interesting. Before producing or consuming a CTI product, ask: *what
decision does this inform?* — block this IP range; deploy DMARC `p=reject` before
the supplier payment run; require MFA on remote access. The test is the "so
what?" question. "Organised cybercrime targets West African banks" is true but
not actionable. "We assess with high confidence that our payment portal is being
profiled using T1598.001; recommend suspending portal access pending MFA" is
actionable.

**Timeliness.** Intelligence delivered after the decision is made is irrelevant.
A CERT-GH advisory received three weeks after the attack wave peaked has little
operational value. The discipline is to produce rapid, high-confidence
statements about the most critical uncertainties first, and complete the broader
picture as time allows.

**Accuracy.** Accuracy is not certainty. It means the product accurately
represents what is *known*, what is *assessed*, and what remains *unknown*. The
most dangerous failure in analytic writing is presenting an assessment as a fact.

### 1.3 The five security properties every analyst must know

Before characterising a threat, know what is being threatened. All of
information security reduces to five properties (CIAAC):

| Property | Meaning | Example breach |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| **Confidentiality** | Only authorised parties can read the data | A customer database is exfiltrated and sold on a dark-web forum |
| **Integrity** | Data is accurate and untampered | An account number in a payment instruction is silently changed, redirecting GHS 2.3M |
| **Availability** | Systems are usable when needed | A DDoS takes mobile banking offline for six hours on a Monday |
| **Authenticity** | An entity/message is genuinely what it claims | A forged email "from the CEO" authorises an urgent transfer |
| **Business continuity** | The organisation can sustain or restore operations | Ransomware encrypts servers *and* backups; there is no tested recovery plan |

When an incident is reported, the first analytic step — before any other — is to
name which property has been violated. That focuses every subsequent decision.

::: warning | A single incident can violate several properties at once
Modern "double-extortion" ransomware destroys **availability** (systems
encrypted), threatens **confidentiality** (data stolen before encryption), and
can violate **integrity** (data corrupted). Always assess all five before
classifying an incident.
:::

## 2 · Know Your Adversary — Threat-Actor Taxonomy

The most fundamental principle of intelligence is that **threats come from
people** — people with motives, constraints, skills, tools and time. "The cyber
threat is evolving" is analytically useless. Useful intelligence identifies
specific actor categories, characterises their methods, and assesses their
relevance to *your* organisation.

### 2.1 Organised cybercriminal groups

Organised cybercrime is the dominant threat to almost every commercial and
government institution in West Africa — the consistent finding of INTERPOL
Africa, Ghana's CSA and the FBI's IC3. Money is the motive, and MoMo networks,
banking systems, payment processors and payroll platforms are high-value targets.

These are **professional organisations**, not teenagers in hoodies — the largest
run affiliate programmes, help desks and "customer service" for ransomware
victims.

- **Motivation:** financial — ransomware, BEC, data theft for sale, fraud-as-a-service.
- **Resourcing:** high and professionalised (Conti, LockBit, ALPHV had revenues in the hundreds of millions before disruption).
- **Patience:** variable — smash-and-grab phishing, or 30–90 day ransomware dwell.
- **Targeting:** opportunistic at recon (scan the internet), selective at monetisation.
- **TTPs:** spearphishing for access; credential theft; lateral movement; exfiltration before encryption; Tor-hosted ransom portals.

::: ghana | 
Ghana's organised-crime threat comes from two directions. First, West African
fraud syndicates have professionalised, running BEC and cryptocurrency fraud at
scale. Second, international ransomware affiliates have begun targeting Ghanaian
financial institutions, drawn by digital-banking growth and limited forensic
capability. The 2022 CSSPS disruption and multiple reported BEC incidents against
Ghanaian exporters are consistent with both streams.

**Act 1038** requires significant incidents be reported to the CSA within **72
hours** — failure to report is itself an offence. Criminals count on victims
staying silent; reporting breaks that calculation and enables sector-wide warnings.
:::

### 2.2 Nation-state actors and APTs

Nation-state actors are the highest-sophistication end of the landscape — funded
by intelligence and military budgets, pursuing espionage, economic advantage,
political disruption or sabotage over long time horizons. "APT" (Advanced
Persistent Threat) was coined by the U.S. Air Force in 2006: **A**dvanced (custom
malware, zero-days), **P**ersistent (long-term, stealthy access), **T**hreat
(deliberate and targeted, not opportunistic).

| Group | Attributed state | Objectives | Notable operations |
|-------|------------------|-----------|--------------------|
| APT28 (Fancy Bear) | Russia (GRU) | Political espionage, disinformation | DNC hack (2016) |
| APT29 (Cozy Bear) | Russia (SVR) | Long-dwell espionage | SolarWinds supply-chain compromise |
| Lazarus Group | North Korea (RGB) | Revenue, sabotage | Bangladesh Bank SWIFT heist ($81M); WannaCry; Sony |
| APT41 (Winnti) | China (MSS/PLA) | Espionage + financial | Software build-system compromises |
| APT34 (OilRig) | Iran (MOIS) | Regional espionage | Gulf energy/government spearphishing |
| Sandworm | Russia (GRU 74455) | Infrastructure sabotage | Ukraine power grid; NotPetya ($10B+ damage) |

The direct nation-state threat to most Ghanaian organisations is lower than to
Western institutions, but not zero: pan-African espionage targeting
resource-extraction contracts, diplomatic communications and peacekeeping
intelligence is documented. **KAIPTC itself** — a peacekeeping training and
research centre with wide international relationships — is precisely the kind of
institution intelligence services seek to penetrate. The more realistic indirect
threat is nation-state *tools* trickling down to organised crime after seizures
or disclosure.

### 2.3 Hacktivists

Motivated by ideology or publicity, operating as loose collectives — Anonymous,
KillNet (pro-Russia DDoS), Sudan-affiliated groups defacing African government
sites. For Ghanaian institutions, hacktivist risk rises around elections, major
announcements, or controversies with international resonance.

### 2.4 Insider threats

The most underestimated category, especially where organisational culture does
not support formal insider-threat programmes. A **malicious** insider acts with
intent (theft, sabotage, facilitating external fraud); a **negligent** insider
causes equivalent damage without intent (clicks a phish, reuses a password,
misconfigures a cloud bucket to public). Indicators — unusual after-hours access,
large downloads, access outside job scope, expressed grievances — are never
definitive and are culturally sensitive, but warrant examination. Not all threats
originate outside the perimeter.

### 2.5 Opportunists and automated scanners

"Script kiddie" is dismissive; the precise description is **low-sophistication,
high-volume, indiscriminate**. The defining characteristic is that they scan the
*entire internet continuously*. Shodan and Censys index every public IP
repeatedly; anything you expose receives automated probes within hours.

::: key | 
Opportunists set the **baseline threat level** for every internet-connected
organisation, regardless of size, sector or location. The question is never "are
we being scanned?" — you are, always — but "does our exposed surface give a
scanner anything to act on?" Common targets: unpatched VPN appliances, exposed
RDP (3389), default-credential routers/cameras, open database ports (3306, 5432,
6379), web apps with known CVEs.
:::

## 3 · The Four Frameworks Professionals Use

A framework is a shared vocabulary that lets practitioners communicate
precisely. Without them, two analysts describing the same attack produce
incompatible reports. These four are the lingua franca of global cybersecurity —
you will meet them in every vendor report, CERT advisory and job interview.

### 3.1 The Cyber Kill Chain (Lockheed Martin, 2011)

Models an intrusion as seven sequential stages. The core insight is defensive:
the adversary must complete every stage to succeed, so disrupting *any* stage
breaks the chain — and the earlier, the cheaper.

| Stage | Description & defensive implication |
|-------|-------------------------------------|
| 1. Reconnaissance | Gather target info — staff, tech, ranges, emails. Passive and equally available to the defender. *Run it on yourself first.* |
| 2. Weaponization | Build the weapon (malicious doc, exploit, phishing page). Not directly observable. |
| 3. Delivery | The weapon reaches the target (email, link, USB, drive-by). Richest detection signal. |
| 4. Exploitation | The weapon executes (macro runs, credential submitted). Patching, allow-listing, training. |
| 5. Installation | Persistence established (backdoor, scheduled task, run key). EDR, file-integrity monitoring. |
| 6. Command & Control | The implant calls home. DNS filtering, proxy inspection, IDS/IPS. |
| 7. Actions on Objectives | The mission: exfiltration, ransomware, fraud, sabotage. Cost rises fast — earlier detection is always cheaper. |

Limitations: it models intrusions as linear; sophisticated actors restart,
pivot, or run parallel chains, and it suits perimeter intrusions better than
insider threats or cloud misconfigurations. Use it as a lens, not a template.

### 3.2 MITRE ATT&CK

The most comprehensive, widely-adopted catalogue of *observed* adversary
behaviour. Organised as **Tactics** (the "why", `TA####`) and **Techniques** (the
"how", `T####`, often with sub-techniques).

The Reconnaissance tactic (**TA0043**), most relevant here:

| Technique | Covers |
|-----------|--------|
| T1589 Gather Victim Identity Information | Names, emails, credentials, employee lists |
| T1590 Gather Victim Network Information | DNS records, IP ranges, topology |
| T1591 Gather Victim Org Information | Business relationships, supply chain, leadership |
| T1593 Search Open Websites/Domains | LinkedIn, social media, job posts, news |
| T1596 Search Open Technical Databases | Shodan, Certificate Transparency, WHOIS, RIRs |
| T1597 Search Closed Sources | Dark-web forums, breach databases |
| T1598 Phishing for Information | Active bait to elicit creds/info (gathering, not malware delivery) |

When a report says "the actor used T1055 Process Injection", every analyst knows
exactly what was done, what it achieves and what detects it. ATT&CK notation is
not optional for a practitioner.

::: key | 
The **ATT&CK Navigator** (attack.mitre.org) lets you highlight techniques,
compare actor profiles and build coverage heat maps. Use it to ask: which
techniques does APT29 use? Which do we detect? The gap is your priority list.
:::

### 3.3 The Diamond Model (Caltagirone, Pendergast & Betz, 2013)

Frames every intrusion event as four linked vertices. Its power is *pivoting*:
from one known vertex you reason toward the others.

- **Adversary** — who is behind this? Motive? Other operations? (usually the goal of the investigation)
- **Capability** — the tool/technique/malware/CVE used.
- **Infrastructure** — domains, IPs, C2, VPN exits. Often reused across operations — the most fertile pivot point.
- **Victim** — who was targeted, and why? Who else fits the profile?

::: case | Diamond Model in practice
A phishing email to a Ghanaian bank links to a credential page on a domain
registered three days earlier (**Capability**). WHOIS shows the registrant email
also registered six similar domains (**Infrastructure**), previously used against
other West African banks. You now have a hypothesis about the **Adversary** (a
financially-motivated actor targeting West African banks) and can warn
profile-matching **Victims** proactively — before the current attack resolves.
:::

### 3.4 The Pyramid of Pain (David Bianco, 2013)

Ranks indicators by one criterion: how much does blocking it *hurt* the adversary?

| Indicator (bottom → top) | Pain to adversary |
|--------------------------|-------------------|
| Hash values | Trivial — one byte changes the hash. Catches the already-seen, not the new. |
| IP addresses | Easy — rotated constantly; a 15-minute change. |
| Domain names | Moderate — minutes to change, but reputation is stickier; DGAs defeat it. |
| Network/host artefacts | Painful — strings, registry keys, mutexes; changing them means rewriting code. |
| Tools | Very painful — abandoning Cobalt Strike for a replacement is months of work. |
| TTPs | Maximum — changing methodology means retraining teams and rebuilding playbooks. This is where ATT&CK operates. |

The consequence: build detection as high up the pyramid as you can. A SOC that
blocks IPs all day is losing; a SOC that detects "credential dumping via LSASS
access followed by lateral movement with stolen hashes" catches the adversary
across campaigns and tools.

## 4 · Indicators, Intelligence Levels & Source Grading

### 4.1 IOCs vs IOAs

**Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)** are forensic evidence that something bad has
happened or is happening — file hashes, C2 IPs, persistence registry keys,
phishing domains. **Indicators of Attack (IOAs)** describe *behaviour*
independent of tools — "a process spawned a child that injected code into a
system process which opened an outbound connection on a non-standard port."

| | IOC | IOA |
|--|-----|-----|
| Focus | What was found (artefact) | What is happening (behaviour) |
| Timing | Backward-looking | Real-time |
| Shelf life | Short — rotated | Durable |
| Pyramid level | Low (hashes, IPs, domains) | High (TTPs) |
| Coverage | Known threats only | Novel variants, zero-days |

Use both: IOCs for automated blocking of known-bad at scale; IOAs for
behavioural detection that survives when the adversary changes tools.

### 4.2 The four levels of threat intelligence

The commonest production failure is writing to the wrong audience.

- **Strategic** (board, C-suite): trends, geopolitical context, long-term risk, legal obligations, financial/reputational impact. No jargon. Months–years.
- **Operational** (CISO, IR lead): who is targeting your sector now, likely attack sequence, detection gaps, urgent controls. Weeks–months.
- **Tactical** (detection engineers, hunters): specific TTPs to hunt, signatures to build, rules to tune. Days–weeks.
- **Technical** (SOC, firewalls, TIP): specific indicators — IPs, domains, hashes, YARA/Sigma rules. Hours–days; often machine-consumed.

::: key | Traffic Light Protocol (TLP)
Every intelligence product carries a sharing marking. **TLP:RED** — named
recipients only. **TLP:AMBER** — your organisation and partners. **TLP:GREEN** —
your community/sector ISAC. **TLP:CLEAR** (formerly WHITE) — public.
:::

### 4.3 Source grading — reliability vs credibility

Two products can contradict each other. Structured grading separates two
questions that are often confused: how reliable is the *source*, and how credible
is this specific *information*? The **Admiralty (NATO)** system grades them
separately — source `A`–`F` (reliable → cannot be judged) and information `1`–`6`
(confirmed → cannot be judged). A reliable source (A/B) can still pass on a
rumour (4/5); an unreliable source can occasionally report something confirmed
(1/2). In practice this is simplified to **High / Medium / Low** confidence — with
the rule that every rating is justified in the text.

::: warning | The two deadliest analytic failures
**Mirror-imaging** — assuming the adversary thinks and acts the way you would.
Actors in different cultural, organisational and economic contexts make decisions
you would not. **Crying wolf** — issuing high-confidence assessments that prove
wrong; one or two destroy a team's credibility for months. When confidence is
medium or low, *say so* — it protects your credibility and calibrates the reader.
:::

## 5 · Passive Reconnaissance — Theory and Practice

### 5.1 The legal and ethical boundary

**Passive** reconnaissance gathers information from public sources without
touching the target's systems — legal, ethical, and available to attacker and
defender alike. **Active** reconnaissance interacts with the target's systems
(port scans) and, without written authorisation, is a criminal offence under
**Act 1038** and its equivalents everywhere. In this module you run active tools
*only* against the isolated `labctl` range.

The rule: if your tool sends a packet to the target's own IP range, it is active.
DNS queries to public resolvers are passive; WHOIS queries are passive; port
scans are always active.

### 5.2 The DNS deep dive

DNS is the internet's address book — and for the analyst, far more: it reveals an
organisation's infrastructure, email architecture and security posture, free, in
seconds, without touching their systems.

| Record | Function | Intelligence value |
|--------|----------|--------------------|
| A / AAAA | Domain → IPv4 / IPv6 | The IP infrastructure; pivot to co-hosted domains |
| MX | Mail servers | Reveals the email provider (Google, M365, on-prem) |
| TXT | Free-text strings | Holds SPF, DMARC pointers, DKIM selectors, verification tokens |
| NS | Authoritative name servers | The DNS hosting provider |
| CNAME | Alias | Reveals CDNs, SaaS, vendor relationships |
| SOA | Zone admin info | The admin contact email — a phishing target |
| CAA | Which CAs may issue certs | A missing CAA means any CA can issue |
| PTR | Reverse DNS (IP → name) | IP-to-domain relationships |

```Terminal — DNS intelligence series (lab)
$ dig lab-corp.com.gh A +short            # IPv4 address
$ dig lab-corp.com.gh MX +short           # mail servers + priority
$ dig lab-corp.com.gh NS +short           # authoritative name servers
$ dig lab-corp.com.gh TXT +short          # SPF + DMARC pointers
$ dig _dmarc.lab-corp.com.gh TXT +short   # DMARC policy
$ dig default._domainkey.lab-corp.com.gh TXT  # DKIM public key
$ dig lab-corp.com.gh SOA +short          # zone admin contact
$ dig lab-corp.com.gh CAA +short          # certificate authorisation
```

**Reading the output — the interpretation layer.** Document every finding as a
verified indicator (record type, value, date, assessment), then interpret:

- `MX → aspmx.l.google.com` → the target uses Google Workspace → phishing pages should mimic Google login.
- `SPF ends in ~all` (soft fail) → forged mail is still delivered → DMARC is the critical control.
- **No `_dmarc` record** → no policy → the domain is effectively spoofable regardless of SPF.
- `NS → aws` → DNS on Route53 → AWS credential compromise could allow DNS hijacking.
- `SOA email` → the DNS administrator's direct contact is now a spearphishing target.

### 5.3 WHOIS intelligence

WHOIS is the registration database of the internet — registrant, organisation,
registration/expiry dates, registrar, name servers. Privacy law redacts much
personal data now, but corporate and historic registrations often retain it.

```Terminal — WHOIS investigation
$ whois lab-corp.com.gh                       # full registration record
$ whois lab-corp.com.gh | grep -Ei 'creat|expir|registr'
$ whois -h whois.afrinic.net 41.210.15.21     # AFRINIC — the RIR for Africa
```

Critical data points: a **registration date** of last week on a domain
impersonating an established brand is a screaming red flag (domain squatting
ahead of a BEC campaign); the **registrant email** is a pivot across other
registrations; shared **name servers** link infrastructure.

::: case | GCB Bank BEC — the reconnaissance phase
The 2021 GCB Bank BEC (GHS 2.3M) began with a recon phase that would have been
*detectable*. Post-incident analysis showed: the attack domain was registered
**21 days** before the first fraudulent email; the registrant email had
registered four other look-alike domains targeting West African banks that year;
and the attack domain's MX pointed to a free email provider — immediately
distinguishing it from the legitimate `gcb.com.gh`. A passive monitoring
programme — new-domain-registration alerting plus Certificate Transparency
monitoring, both free and passive — would have surfaced the infrastructure *weeks*
before it was used.
:::

### 5.4 theHarvester — OSINT at scale

`theHarvester` automates collection of email addresses, subdomains and IPs about
a domain from search engines, Certificate Transparency, Shodan and more.

```Terminal — theHarvester
$ theHarvester -d lab-corp.com.gh -b all -l 200 -f report
$ theHarvester -d lab-corp.com.gh -b crtsh      # certificate transparency only
$ cat report.json | python3 -m json.tool | less
```

From the attacker's view the output is a targeting list; from the defender's it
is an asset list that needs protection — every exposed email is a phishing victim
who needs training, every subdomain an attack surface for the inventory.

## 6 · Email Authentication — SPF, DKIM, DMARC & BIMI

Email authentication is the single most impactful control available to any
organisation for **zero additional cost** — and the most misunderstood area of
this module. Most BEC, domain-spoofing phishing and impersonation fraud (the
dominant financial-fraud vector in West Africa) depends on the target having
failed to deploy these three DNS-based controls.

### 6.1 Why email is fundamentally broken

Email was designed in 1971 for a world with no adversaries. SMTP, which still
underlies all email, was built with complete trust: a server could claim to send
from any domain and the receiver had no way to verify it — like posting a letter
with a fake return address. The `From:` header the recipient sees can be set to
any value. Without authentication, nothing stops an attacker setting
`From: ceo@yourbank.com` and sending from a server anywhere.

### 6.2 SPF — Sender Policy Framework

A TXT record listing which servers may send for the domain, ending in a
qualifier: `-all` (**hard fail** — reject everything else), `~all` (**soft fail**
— "probably forged, deliver anyway"), `?all` (neutral — worthless).

`v=spf1 ip4:41.210.15.21 include:_spf.google.com -all`

SPF's weakness: it validates the *envelope* sender (Return-Path), not the visible
`From:` header — so SPF alone does not stop display-domain spoofing.

### 6.3 DKIM — DomainKeys Identified Mail

The sending server signs the message with a private key; the public key is
published in DNS at a *selector* (`selector._domainkey.domain`). The receiver
verifies the signature — proving the message was genuinely sent by the domain and
not altered in transit. DKIM survives forwarding where SPF often breaks.

### 6.4 DMARC — the policy layer that ties it together

Published at `_dmarc.domain`:
`v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@domain; pct=100`

Two things make DMARC decisive:

- **Policy (`p`)** — `none` (monitor only — *no protection*), `quarantine` (spam folder), or `reject` (refuse outright).
- **Alignment** — DMARC requires the SPF/DKIM-validated domain to *match the visible `From:` domain*. This is what finally stops display-domain spoofing, which SPF and DKIM alone do not.

The `rua` address receives aggregate reports — invaluable for seeing who is
sending as you. **No DMARC record, or `p=none`, means the domain is effectively
spoofable.** Publishing `p=reject` is free and is the single highest-value
anti-phishing control for your own brand.

::: warning | The exact combination to look for
A domain with **SPF `~all`** *and* **no DMARC** is wide open. The soft-fail
delivers forged mail, and with no policy there is nothing to reject it and no
alignment check. This is the precise exposure you will confirm in the lab — and
the exact gap behind most Ghanaian BEC.
:::

### 6.5 BIMI — the incentive layer

Brand Indicators for Message Identification lets a domain publish its logo (via a
`default._bimi` TXT record and a verified mark certificate) to display beside
authenticated mail in supporting clients. BIMI **requires DMARC at enforcement
(`quarantine`/`reject`)** — so it is both a brand-trust feature and a business
incentive to finally deploy DMARC properly.

## 7 · Writing the Threat Brief

Findings are worthless until a decision-maker can act on them. A professional
brief is **BLUF-first** (Bottom Line Up Front):

1. **BLUF** — the key judgement and recommended action, in the first two sentences.
2. **Verified indicators (facts)** — each with its source and the date retrieved.
3. **Assessment (hypotheses)** — each labelled with a confidence level and its basis.
4. **Recommended actions** — specific, prioritised, owned.
5. **Knowledge gaps** — an honest statement of what you do *not* yet know.

Write to the audience level (§4.2). The three failures that destroy a brief:
**presenting an assessment as a fact**; **burying the decision** at the bottom;
and **omitting confidence levels** so the reader cannot calibrate.

::: note | Worked BLUF
"We assess with **medium confidence** that our supplier-payment domain is being
prepared for a spoofing campaign, based on a look-alike domain registered on

## Key takeaways

- CTI is **actionable, timely, accurate** intelligence — the product of a cycle, not a pile of data.
- Speak in frameworks: **Kill Chain** (where in the intrusion), **ATT&CK** (which technique), **Diamond** (how to pivot), **Pyramid of Pain** (how durable the indicator).
- **IOC = what happened; IOA = behaviour now.** Push detection up the pyramid, toward TTPs.
- Write to the right **level** and tag **confidence**; keep fact separate from assessment.
- **No DMARC / `p=none` = spoofable.** `-all` + DKIM + DMARC `p=reject` (with alignment) is the goal.
- Recon techniques: **T1589 / T1590 / T1598**, under tactic **TA0043**.

## Check yourself

1. **A domain publishes `v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all` and has no `_dmarc` record. How exposed is it, and why?**
   → Highly exposed. `~all` only soft-fails forged mail (still delivered), and with no DMARC there is no policy to reject it and no alignment check — an attacker can send mail that appears to come from the domain and it will land.

2. **Rank by the Pyramid of Pain: blocking a file hash, a C2 IP, or forcing a change of techniques.**
   → TTPs (most pain) > C2 IP > file hash (least — trivially recompiled). Aim defence as high as possible.

3. **Give an IOC and an IOA for a brute-force login.**
   → IOC: a known-bad source IP in the logs. IOA: "many failed authentications from one source followed by a success."

4. **Which is passive: `nmap -sV target` or `dig target MX`? Why does it matter legally?**
   → `dig` — it reads public DNS, never touching the target. `nmap -sV` actively probes the target, which without written authorisation is an offence under Act 1038.

5. **What makes "we assess with medium confidence that our supplier domain is being prepared for spoofing" a professional statement rather than alarmism?**
   → It is labelled an *assessment* (not a fact) and carries an explicit *confidence level*, followed by the verified facts and a recommended action.

## Glossary

- **CTI** — cyber threat intelligence: evidence-led understanding of adversaries.
- **Intelligence cycle** — Planning → Collection → Processing → Analysis → Dissemination.
- **Cyber Kill Chain** — the 7-stage model of an intrusion.
- **MITRE ATT&CK** — catalogue of tactics (`TA…`) and techniques (`T…`).
- **Diamond Model** — adversary · capability · infrastructure · victim.
- **Pyramid of Pain** — indicators ranked by how much blocking them hurts the attacker.
- **IOC / IOA** — indicator of compromise / of attack.
- **SPF / DKIM / DMARC / BIMI** — authorised-sender list / signature / enforcement policy with alignment / brand-logo incentive layer.
- **TLP** — Traffic Light Protocol for controlling intelligence sharing.
- **Admiralty grading** — grading source reliability and information credibility separately.
- **CERT-GH / CSA** — Ghana's national incident-response team / Cyber Security Authority.
