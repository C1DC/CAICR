# Module 6 — Digital Forensics & Incident Response

**Day 3 · Afternoon · Hands-on lab: `labctl start m6`**

In one sentence: when an incident happens, the evidence is fragile and the clock
is running — this module teaches you to preserve what happened so it stands up
later, reconstruct it, and respond without making things worse.

By the end you will be able to:

- examine a disk image, list files and recover deleted ones;
- decode an obfuscated attacker artefact;
- place your actions correctly in the NIST incident-response lifecycle.

---

## The big picture

Digital forensics rests on one idea: **prove you didn't change the evidence.**
If you can't, nothing you find is trustworthy — not to a court, a regulator, or
your own board. Two disciplines make that possible:

- **Hashing.** A cryptographic hash (SHA-256) is a digital fingerprint of a file.
  Hash the evidence *before* you touch it and *after*; if the two fingerprints
  match, you've mathematically proven it's unchanged. Change a single byte and
  the hash changes completely.
- **Chain of custody.** A written record of who held the evidence, when, why, and
  what they did. It's what makes your findings *defensible*.

A second principle governs *what to collect first*: the **order of volatility.**
Some evidence vanishes faster than the rest. Memory (RAM) disappears the instant
you power off; network connections and running processes go next; disk persists.
So you collect in that order — **volatile first** — which is exactly why "just
turn it off and reboot" destroys the most valuable evidence.

**Incident response (IR)** is the disciplined process wrapped around all this,
standardised by **NIST SP 800-61**.

## An analogy

It's a crime scene. The first officer doesn't start moving furniture — they
photograph everything, bag each item, label it, and log who touched what. A
fingerprint is only evidence if you can show it wasn't planted or smudged. A disk
image is the same: you make an exact copy, fingerprint it (hash), and work on the
*copy*, never the original — so the original stays pristine and provable. And
just as you'd collect a dying witness's statement before anything else, you
capture volatile memory before it's gone.

## How it actually works

**Preserve first:**

```
sha256sum evidence.img                 # fingerprint it
sha256sum -c evidence.sha256           # later: prove it still matches
```

**Examine** with Autopsy (a GUI forensic platform used by law enforcement) or its
command-line engine, Sleuthkit:

- `fsstat` — what filesystem is this, and how is it laid out?
- `fls` — list files; a `*` marks a **deleted** entry.
- `icat` — read a file by its internal id (its *inode*), including deleted ones.
- `tsk_recover` — carve deleted files back out.

Why can you recover "deleted" files? Deleting usually just marks the space as
free and flags the directory entry — the actual data often sits on disk untouched
until something overwrites it. Forensics exploits that gap.

**Decode** obfuscated artefacts with **CyberChef** (or the command line).
Attackers hide payloads in layers — Base64, URL-encoding, hex. You peel them back
("From Base64 → URL Decode") to reveal, say, the malware's command-and-control
URL.

Then place your work in the **NIST SP 800-61 lifecycle:**

| Phase | What you do |
|-------|-------------|
| **Preparation** | Policies, playbooks, trained team, tested tools — *before* anything happens |
| **Detection & Analysis** | Confirm it's real, scope it, start the evidence timeline |
| **Containment** | Stop the bleeding *without destroying evidence* (isolate, don't power off) |
| **Eradication** | Remove the attacker — close the vector, reset credentials, kill persistence |
| **Recovery** | Restore from clean backups, verify, reconnect |
| **Post-Incident** | Lessons learned, improve controls, report if required |

## Ghana in focus

In a **2023 West African hospital ransomware** incident, three failures turned a
problem into a catastrophe. The backups were on the *same network segment* and
got encrypted too (no offline copy). The IT team's instinct was to **power the
servers off immediately** — destroying the volatile memory evidence and making
recovery harder. And there was no tested incident-response plan, so the response
was improvised under pressure. The result: a three-week outage and partially
unrecoverable patient records. The antidote is the **3-2-1 backup rule** — 3
copies, 2 media types, 1 offline/offsite — with **restores tested monthly**, plus
an IR plan practised *before* it's needed.

## Why it matters

- Evidence that isn't preserved properly is worthless when it matters most — in
  court, with the CSA/DPC, or in an insurance claim.
- Recovering deleted files and decoding artefacts is often how you learn what the
  attacker actually did and took.
- Following the lifecycle keeps a panicked response from destroying evidence or
  reinfecting clean systems.

## Common mistakes

- **Powering off to "stop the attack".** You destroy RAM evidence; isolate from
  the network instead.
- **Working on the original.** Always image and hash first; analyse the copy.
- **Skipping the hash.** Without before/after hashes you cannot prove integrity.
- **Containment that wipes evidence.** Stop the spread, but preserve the scene.
- **Backups on the same network.** Ransomware encrypts those too — you need an
  offline copy.

## Key takeaways

- **Hash before and after** — mathematical proof the evidence is unchanged.
- **Chain of custody** makes findings defensible.
- **Order of volatility: memory first**, disk last — never just power off.
- "Deleted" ≠ gone; `tsk_recover`/`icat` carve files back.
- NIST SP 800-61: **Prepare → Detect → Contain → Eradicate → Recover →
  Post-Incident.**
- **3-2-1 backups, tested monthly.**

## Check yourself

1. **You arrive at a compromised server that's still running. First instinct:
   pull the power. Why is that wrong?**
   → It destroys volatile memory (RAM) evidence — running processes, injected
   code, keys. Isolate from the network instead, then capture memory before disk.

2. **You hash the image before analysis and again after. The hashes match. What
   has that proven?**
   → That your analysis did not alter the evidence — its integrity (and your
   chain of custody) holds.

3. **`fls` shows a file with a `*`. What does that mean, and can you read it?**
   → It's deleted; yes — the data usually persists, so `icat`/`tsk_recover` can
   recover it.

4. **Containment vs eradication — the difference?**
   → Containment stops the spread (isolate the host) while preserving evidence;
   eradication removes the attacker (close the vector, reset creds, remove
   persistence).

## Glossary

- **Hash (SHA-256)** — a file's cryptographic fingerprint.
- **Chain of custody** — the documented handling history of evidence.
- **Order of volatility** — collect the most perishable evidence (RAM) first.
- **Autopsy / Sleuthkit** — forensic platform / its CLI toolkit.
- **inode** — a file's internal id in a filesystem.
- **NIST SP 800-61** — the incident-response lifecycle standard.
- **3-2-1 rule** — 3 copies, 2 media, 1 offsite/offline.
