# Module 9 — Cyber Governance & Business Continuity

**Day 5 · Morning · Leadership module (no Kali lab — you brief a board)**

In one sentence: everything from the week only matters if leadership can
*govern* it — set direction, demand assurance, measure what counts, and keep the
institution running through a crisis — and this module teaches you to speak that
language.

By the end you will be able to:
- distinguish governance from management and place accountability correctly;
- choose metrics a board should actually care about;
- set recovery priorities with Business Impact Analysis, RTO and RPO;
- write a one-page board memo and run a crisis-command structure.

---

## The big picture

**Governance is not management.** Governance is *setting direction and demanding
assurance* — the board decides risk appetite, approves policy and budget, and
asks "are we actually protected?" Management is *doing the work* — the CISO and
teams implement, operate and report. Confusing the two is a classic failure:
when security is treated as "the IT department's job", no one at the top owns the
risk, and Act 1038 now makes that a legal problem, not just a bad habit.

A clean way to see accountability is the **three lines model**:

- **First line** — operations *own and run* the risk day to day.
- **Second line** — risk and compliance *oversee and challenge*.
- **Third line** — internal audit *provides independent assurance*.

Governance also sets the **policy hierarchy**, from general to specific:
**Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.** A policy says "we protect data"; a
standard says "AES-256 and MFA"; a procedure says "here are the exact steps"; a
guideline offers helpful advice.

## An analogy

Think of a ship. The **board** is the owner setting the destination and how much
rough weather is acceptable (risk appetite). The **captain** (CISO) sails the
ship, reports position, and raises the alarm. The owner doesn't steer — but they
*do* insist on knowing whether the lifeboats work, whether the crew has drilled,
and whether the last inspection passed. A board that only hears "all fine,
trust me" and never demands evidence is an owner who finds out the lifeboats
leak when the ship is already going down.

## How it actually works

Boards drown in technical detail and starve for meaning. Give them **metrics that
translate security into business risk:**

| Metric | What it measures | Why the board cares |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|
| Patch compliance rate | % of critical fixes applied within SLA | Unpatched = open doors |
| MFA adoption | % of accounts with MFA | The single highest-impact control vs. credential theft |
| Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) | Attack start → detection | Longer = more damage |
| Mean Time to Respond (MTTR) | Detection → containment | Longer = more cost |
| Phishing-sim failure rate | % of staff who click | Measures the human firewall; should trend down |
| Backup restore test result | Last *verified* successful restore | A failed restore = no disaster recovery |

Then **business continuity**, which answers "how do we survive and recover?":

- **Business Impact Analysis (BIA)** — for each critical service, what fails
  downstream if it goes down? This ranks what to recover first.
- **RTO (Recovery Time Objective)** — the maximum acceptable *downtime* for a
  service.
- **RPO (Recovery Point Objective)** — the maximum acceptable *data loss* (how far
  back your last good backup is).
- **Crisis command** — a clear structure: an **Incident Commander** over a
  **Technical** cell, a **Communications** cell, and a **Legal/Privacy** cell —
  with a **decision log** recording every call, who made it, and why. That log is
  your audit trail afterwards.

## Ghana in focus

When a national system fails — recall the CSSPS disruption from Module 2 — the
questions that decide whether it's a bad day or a disaster are *governance*
questions asked long before: Had the board set an RTO for the service? Was there
a tested recovery plan and an owner? Were backups verified? Institutions that
answer "yes" recover in hours; those that answer "we assumed IT had it" recover
in weeks and headlines. A board that reviews the six metrics above each quarter —
especially the **backup restore test** — is doing the single most valuable thing
a board can do for cyber resilience.

## Why it matters

- Clear governance means someone *accountable* owns cyber risk before a crisis,
  not after.
- The right metrics let non-technical leaders make good funding and risk
  decisions.
- RTO/RPO agreed in advance turn a chaotic outage into an orderly, prioritised
  recovery.

## Common mistakes

- **"Security is IT's problem."** It's a board accountability under Act 1038.
- **Drowning the board in jargon.** Report business risk and the six metrics, not
  CVE numbers.
- **Untested backups.** An unverified backup is a hope, not a control — the restore
  test is the metric that matters.
- **No decision log in a crisis.** Without it you cannot explain or defend your
  choices afterwards.

## Put it to work

In the course you take conflicting reports of a critical outage and produce an
**executive cyber-risk memo** and a **service recovery priority table** with
RTO/RPO — the deliverable you defend before an executive panel, and the leadership
backbone of the Module 10 capstone.

## Key takeaways

- **Governance sets direction and demands assurance; management executes.**
- **Three lines**: operations own risk, risk/compliance oversee, audit assures.
- Policy hierarchy: **Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.**
- Report **board-level metrics** (MFA %, MTTD/MTTR, patch rate, restore tests).
- **BIA + RTO + RPO** drive recovery; a **decision log** is your crisis audit
  trail.

## Check yourself

1. **The board asks "are we secure?" What kind of answer shows good governance?**
   → Evidence, not reassurance: the six metrics (MFA adoption, MTTD/MTTR, patch
   compliance, last verified restore, phishing-sim rate) and the top residual
   risks — so they can decide, not just trust.

2. **RTO vs RPO?**
   → RTO = maximum acceptable downtime; RPO = maximum acceptable data loss (how
   old the last recoverable backup can be).

3. **Governance or management: approving the cybersecurity budget and risk
   appetite?**
   → Governance (the board). Implementing the controls that spend it is
   management.

4. **Why is the backup *restore test* more important than the backup itself?**
   → A backup you've never restored may not work; only a verified restore proves
   you actually have disaster recovery.

## Glossary

- **Governance vs management** — directing and assuring vs executing.
- **Three lines model** — operations / risk & compliance / internal audit.
- **Policy hierarchy** — Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.
- **MTTD / MTTR** — mean time to detect / to respond.
- **BIA** — Business Impact Analysis.
- **RTO / RPO** — max downtime / max data loss per service.
- **Decision log** — timestamped record of crisis decisions and rationale.
