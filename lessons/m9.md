## 1 · Governance Is Direction and Assurance

**Governance is not management.** Governance *sets direction and demands
assurance* — the board decides risk appetite, approves policy and budget, and
asks "are we actually protected?" Management *does the work* — the CISO and teams
implement, operate and report. Confusing the two is the classic failure: when
security is "IT's problem", no one at the top owns the risk — and **Act 1038** now
makes that a legal problem, not merely a bad habit.

**NIST CSF 2.0's GOVERN function** and **ISO/IEC 27001** (the information-security
management-system standard) both exist to make this accountability explicit and
auditable.

The **three lines model** places accountability cleanly:

- **First line** — operations *own and run* the risk day to day.
- **Second line** — risk and compliance *oversee and challenge*.
- **Third line** — internal audit provides *independent assurance*.

::: key | The governance failure Act 1038 targets
"Cybersecurity is the IT department's problem." Under Act 1038 it is a board
accountability. A board with no standing cyber agenda item, no named accountable
executive, and no approved policy is in a governance gap a regulator can act on.

### 1.1 The CISO and the risk appetite

Good governance needs **a named, accountable executive** — a **CISO** (or, in a
smaller institution, a designated security lead) with the authority and the budget
to act, and **direct access to the board**. The reporting line matters: a CISO
buried three levels under the people whose systems they must challenge cannot do
the job. The board's own instrument is a **risk-appetite statement** — a plain
sentence of how much risk the institution will accept to pursue its goals (for
example, *"we will not accept any risk to the confidentiality of member financial
data, and no more than four hours' downtime on benefit payments"*). Appetite set
at the top is what lets everyone below prioritise consistently. (**COBIT** is the
IT-governance framework that formalises this operating model.)

## 2 · The Policy Hierarchy

From general to specific — each layer gives effect to the one above:

- **Policy** — "we protect personal and institutional data" (what & why, board-approved).
- **Standard** — "AES-256 encryption; MFA on all remote access" (mandatory specifics).
- **Procedure** — "here are the exact steps to enrol MFA" (how).
- **Guideline** — "advice on choosing a strong passphrase" (recommended, not mandatory).

A control that lives only in someone's head is not governance. It must trace up
to an approved policy and down to a repeatable procedure.

---

## 3 · Metrics That Matter to a Board

Boards drown in technical detail and starve for meaning. Translate security into
business risk with a small, honest set:

| Metric | What it measures | Why the board cares |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|
| Patch compliance rate | % of critical fixes applied within SLA | Unpatched = open doors |
| MFA adoption | % of accounts with MFA | The single highest-impact control vs credential theft |
| Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) | Attack start → detection | Longer = more damage |
| Mean Time to Respond (MTTR) | Detection → containment | Longer = more cost |
| Phishing-sim failure rate | % of staff who click | The human firewall; should trend down |
| Backup restore test result | Last *verified* successful restore | A failed restore = no disaster recovery |

::: key | The one metric never to skip
The **backup restore test**. A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a
control. This line belongs in every board pack — and Maersk's near-miss (below)
is why.
:::

::: warning | Vanity metrics vs decision-useful metrics
"**Attacks blocked: 4.2 million**", "firewall uptime 99.98%", "antivirus on 100% of
laptops" are **vanity metrics** — big numbers that look reassuring and inform *no
decision*. A board metric is useful only if a bad value triggers an action:
*MTTD rising* → invest in detection; *patch compliance 60%* → fix the patch
process; *last restore test failed* → stop everything. Prefer **leading**
indicators (that warn before harm) over **lagging** ones (that only count damage).
:::

---

## 4 · The Board-Reporting Discipline

Good governance answers "are we secure?" with **evidence, not reassurance**: the
six metrics above, the top residual risks (from the M2 register), the status of
the last incident's lessons, and a clear decision ask. The failures that destroy
a board brief: drowning it in jargon and CVE numbers; presenting an assessment as
a fact (Module 1); and never surfacing the residual risk the board is implicitly
accepting.

## 5 · Business Continuity — Surviving and Recovering

Risk treatment reduces harm; **continuity** keeps you running when harm lands.

### 5.1 The Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

The BIA is the foundation. For each service you ask, methodically: *what does the
business lose, per hour and per day, if this is down?* — financial, regulatory,
safety and reputational — and *what else fails downstream* (its dependencies).
That gives every service a priority and two numbers agreed **with the business**,
not guessed by IT:

- **RTO (Recovery Time Objective)** — the maximum acceptable **downtime**.
- **RPO (Recovery Point Objective)** — the maximum acceptable **data loss**, i.e.
  how far back your last usable backup may be.

### 5.2 Choosing a recovery strategy

The RTO you promised dictates what you must *build* — and the cost rises as the
RTO shrinks:

- **Cold site / restore-from-backup** — cheapest, slowest (days): bare infrastructure, restore everything.
- **Warm site** — partial standby (hours): some systems pre-staged.
- **Hot site / active-active or cloud DR** — fastest (minutes), most expensive: a running replica you fail over to.
- **Manual workaround** — the non-technical fallback (pay pensioners from a pre-agreed list) that buys time while systems recover.

**ISO 22301** is the business-continuity-management standard that formalises all
of this; **3-2-1 backups** (Module 6) are the control that makes any of it real.

### 5.3 Test it, or you don't have it

A plan that has never been exercised is fiction. Rehearse up a ladder of realism:

1. **Tabletop** — the team talks through a scenario in a room. Cheapest; finds the
   obvious gaps.
2. **Walkthrough / simulation** — actually perform the steps in a test environment.
3. **Full failover** — cut production over to the recovery site for real.

Every exercise ends in a **lessons-learned** review that feeds the governance
cycle. The restore test is non-negotiable (Maersk, below).

## 6 · Crisis Command

Borrow the structure emergency services use (an **Incident Command** model):

- an **Incident Commander** who directs and decides;
- a **Technical** cell (investigate, contain, eradicate, recover);
- a **Communications** cell (staff, customers, regulators, press);
- a **Legal/Privacy** cell (Act 843/1038 obligations, counsel);
- and a **decision log** — every decision with a timestamp, the decision-maker and
  the rationale. That log is your audit trail and your defence afterwards.

### 6.1 Crisis communications

How you *speak* in a crisis decides whether you keep trust. Prepare **holding
statements** in advance; communicate **early, honestly and consistently** across
audiences — staff, customers, the **regulator (DPC/CSA)**, partners, and the press
— each with the right message. The worst move is a confident early statement that
later proves false (Module 8: *brief counsel before any external communication*).
Maersk recovered its *reputation*, not just its systems, because its crisis comms
were prompt and honest. Silence and spin both cost more than the breach.

## 7 · Operational Resilience — the Modern Frame

Regulators worldwide (and increasingly Bank of Ghana) are moving past "business
continuity" to **operational resilience**: not *"can this system recover?"* but
*"can the institution keep delivering the services its customers depend on,
through disruption, whatever the cause?"* Three ideas define it:

- **Important business services** — framed from the *customer's* view (paying a
  pensioner), not the system's (the database).
- **Impact tolerances** — the maximum disruption to each service the institution
  (and its regulator) will tolerate — a harder commitment than an internal RTO.
- **Severe-but-plausible scenarios** — you test resilience against realistic worst
  cases (a cloud-region failure, a ransomware wipe, a key-vendor collapse), not
  just tidy ones.

It reframes everything above around the outcome the board and the regulator
actually care about: *the service stays up for the people who rely on it.*

## 8 · An Analogy

A ship. The **board** is the owner, setting the destination and how much rough
weather is acceptable (risk appetite). The **captain** (CISO) sails the ship,
reports position, and raises the alarm. The owner does not steer — but they
*insist* on knowing whether the lifeboats work, whether the crew has drilled, and
whether the last inspection passed. A board that only hears "all fine, trust me"
and never demands evidence is an owner who discovers the lifeboats leak when the
ship is already going down.

## 9 · Scenarios — Governance Decides the Outcome

::: ghana | CSSPS — a governance question before it was a technical one
Whether a national-system outage is a bad day or a disaster is decided by
governance questions asked long before: Had the board set an RTO for the service?
Was there a tested recovery plan and an owner? Were backups verified? Institutions
that answer "yes" recover in hours; those that assumed "IT had it" recover in
weeks and headlines.
:::

::: case | Maersk / NotPetya, 2017 — tested recovery, and crisis comms
Maersk survived a total IT wipe-out largely because one domain-controller backup
had been offline during the attack; afterwards it rebuilt resilience
deliberately. **Tested recovery is not optional, and luck is not a strategy.** The
firms that also recover their *reputation* are those whose crisis communications
were honest, prompt and coordinated — a governance capability, not a technical one.
:::

## Key takeaways

- **Governance sets direction and demands assurance; management executes.** Anchored
  by **CSF 2.0 GOVERN**, **ISO 27001**, a named **CISO** and a **risk-appetite statement**.
- **Three lines**: operations own risk, risk/compliance oversee, audit assures.
- Policy hierarchy: **Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.**
- Report **decision-useful** metrics (MFA %, MTTD/MTTR, patch rate, restore tests),
  never **vanity** ones ("attacks blocked").
- **BIA → RTO/RPO → a DR strategy you can afford → and *test* it** (tabletop →
  failover; **ISO 22301**). A backup never restored is a hope.
- A **decision log** and honest **crisis communications** protect you afterwards;
  the modern frame is **operational resilience** — the service stays up for the
  people who rely on it.

## Check yourself

1. **The board asks "are we secure?" What kind of answer shows good governance?**
   → Evidence, not reassurance: the six metrics and the top residual risks, so the board can *decide* rather than just trust.

2. **RTO vs RPO?**
   → RTO = maximum acceptable downtime; RPO = maximum acceptable data loss (how old the last recoverable backup can be).

3. **Governance or management: approving the cybersecurity budget and risk appetite?**
   → Governance (the board). Implementing the controls it funds is management.

4. **Why is the backup *restore test* more important than the backup itself?**
   → A backup you have never restored may not work; only a verified restore proves disaster recovery — as Maersk's near-miss showed.

5. **In a crisis, why keep a decision log?**
   → It records what was decided, when and why — the audit trail you need to explain and defend your choices afterwards.

6. **Your board pack's headline security metric is "4.2 million attacks blocked."
   Why is that weak, and what would you report instead?**
   → It is a **vanity metric** — it informs no decision. Report metrics where a bad
   value triggers an action: **MTTD/MTTR**, patch compliance, MFA adoption, and the
   last **restore-test** result.

7. **The board set a 4-hour RTO for benefit payments; the current capability is 48
   hours. What two things does that tell you to do?**
   → There is a **48-vs-4 gap**, so (a) invest in a faster recovery strategy (warm/
   hot site or cloud DR) to close it, and (b) **test** it — and meanwhile define a
   **manual workaround** to bridge the gap.

## Glossary

- **Governance vs management** — directing and assuring vs executing.
- **CISO / risk appetite** — the accountable security executive / how much risk the board will accept.
- **NIST CSF GOVERN / ISO 27001 / ISO 22301 / COBIT** — governance function / ISMS standard / BC standard / IT-governance framework.
- **Three lines model** — operations / risk & compliance / internal audit.
- **Policy hierarchy** — Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.
- **Vanity vs decision-useful metric** — a reassuring number that drives no action / one where a bad value triggers one.
- **MTTD / MTTR** — mean time to detect / respond.
- **BIA / RTO / RPO** — business-impact analysis / max downtime / max data loss.
- **DR strategy (cold/warm/hot)** — restore-from-backup / partial standby / running replica; cost rises as RTO shrinks.
- **BC testing (tabletop → walkthrough → failover)** — the ladder of exercise realism.
- **Operational resilience** — keeping important business services within impact tolerances through severe-but-plausible disruption.
- **Incident Commander / decision log / crisis comms** — the crisis director / the timestamped record of decisions / honest, coordinated messaging.
