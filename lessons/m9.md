# Module 9 — Cyber Governance & Business Continuity

**Day 5 · Morning · Leadership module (no Kali lab — you brief a board)**

Everything from the week only creates value if leadership can *govern* it — set
direction, demand assurance, measure what matters, and keep the institution
running through a crisis. This module teaches you to translate five days of
technical work into the language of the board, and to build the resilience that
decides whether an incident is a bad day or an existential one.

By the end you will be able to:
- distinguish governance from management and place accountability correctly;
- choose the metrics a board should actually care about;
- set recovery priorities with Business Impact Analysis, RTO and RPO;
- write a one-page board memo and run a crisis-command structure.

---

## 1. The big picture: governance is direction and assurance

**Governance is not management.** Governance *sets direction and demands
assurance* — the board decides risk appetite, approves policy and budget, and
asks "are we actually protected?" Management *does the work* — the CISO and teams
implement, operate and report. Confusing the two is the classic failure: when
security is "IT's problem", no one at the top owns the risk, and **Act 1038** now
makes that a legal problem, not merely a bad habit. **NIST CSF 2.0's GOVERN
function** and **ISO/IEC 27001** (the information-security management-system
standard) both exist to make this accountability explicit and auditable.

The **three lines model** places accountability cleanly:

- **First line** — operations *own and run* the risk day to day.
- **Second line** — risk and compliance *oversee and challenge*.
- **Third line** — internal audit provides *independent assurance*.

And the **policy hierarchy**, general to specific: **Policy → Standard →
Procedure → Guideline.** A policy says "we protect data"; a standard says
"AES-256 and MFA"; a procedure gives the exact steps; a guideline offers advice.

## 2. Metrics that matter to a board

Boards drown in technical detail and starve for meaning. Translate security into
business risk with a small, honest set of metrics:

| Metric | What it measures | Why the board cares |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|
| Patch compliance rate | % of critical fixes applied within SLA | Unpatched = open doors |
| MFA adoption | % of accounts with MFA | The single highest-impact control vs credential theft |
| Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) | Attack start → detection | Longer = more damage |
| Mean Time to Respond (MTTR) | Detection → containment | Longer = more cost |
| Phishing-sim failure rate | % of staff who click | The human firewall; should trend down |
| Backup restore test result | Last *verified* successful restore | A failed restore = no disaster recovery |

A board that reviews these quarterly — especially the **restore test** — is doing
the single most valuable thing a board can do for cyber resilience.

## 3. Business continuity: surviving and recovering

- **Business Impact Analysis (BIA)** — for each critical service, what fails
  downstream if it goes down? This ranks what to recover first.
- **RTO** (Recovery Time Objective) — the maximum acceptable *downtime*.
- **RPO** (Recovery Point Objective) — the maximum acceptable *data loss* (how far
  back the last good backup is).
- **ISO 22301** — the business-continuity-management standard that formalises all
  of this.
- **Crisis command** — a clear structure borrowed from emergency management (an
  **Incident Command** model): an **Incident Commander** over **Technical**,
  **Communications** and **Legal/Privacy** cells, with a **decision log**
  recording every call, who made it, and why. That log is your audit trail
  afterwards.

## 4. An analogy

A ship. The **board** is the owner, setting the destination and how much rough
weather is acceptable (risk appetite). The **captain** (CISO) sails the ship,
reports position, and raises the alarm. The owner does not steer — but they
*insist* on knowing whether the lifeboats work, whether the crew has drilled, and
whether the last inspection passed. A board that only hears "all fine, trust me"
and never demands evidence is an owner who discovers the lifeboats leak when the
ship is already going down.

## 5. Scenarios — governance decides the outcome

### Local (Ghana)

- Recall the **CSSPS disruption** (Module 2). Whether a national-system outage is
  a bad day or a disaster is decided by *governance* questions asked long before:
  Had the board set an RTO for the service? Was there a tested recovery plan and
  an owner? Were backups verified? Institutions that answer "yes" recover in
  hours; those that assumed "IT had it" recover in weeks and headlines.

### International (well-documented)

- **Maersk / NotPetya, 2017 (~US$300M).** The company survived a total IT wipe-out
  largely by chance — a single domain-controller backup had been offline (in a
  Ghana office) during the attack. Afterwards, Maersk rebuilt its resilience
  deliberately. The governance lesson every board now cites: **tested recovery is
  not optional**, and luck is not a strategy.
- Post-incident, the firms that recover reputation are those whose **crisis
  communications** were honest, prompt and coordinated — a governance capability,
  not a technical one.

## 6. Why it matters

- Clear governance means someone *accountable* owns cyber risk before a crisis,
  not after.
- The right metrics let non-technical leaders make good funding and risk
  decisions.
- RTO/RPO agreed in advance turn a chaotic outage into an orderly, prioritised
  recovery.

## 7. Common mistakes

- **"Security is IT's problem."** It is a board accountability under Act 1038.
- **Drowning the board in jargon.** Report business risk and the six metrics, not
  CVE numbers.
- **Untested backups.** An unverified backup is a hope, not a control — the restore
  test is the metric that matters.
- **No decision log in a crisis.** Without it you cannot explain or defend your
  choices afterwards.

## Key takeaways

- **Governance sets direction and demands assurance; management executes.**
  Anchored by **CSF 2.0 GOVERN** and **ISO 27001**.
- **Three lines**: operations own risk, risk/compliance oversee, audit assures.
- Policy hierarchy: **Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.**
- Report **board-level metrics** (MFA %, MTTD/MTTR, patch rate, restore tests).
- **BIA + RTO + RPO** drive recovery (**ISO 22301**); a **decision log** is your
  crisis audit trail.

## Check yourself

1. **The board asks "are we secure?" What kind of answer shows good governance?**
   → Evidence, not reassurance: the six metrics and the top residual risks, so the
   board can *decide* rather than just trust.

2. **RTO vs RPO?**
   → RTO = maximum acceptable downtime; RPO = maximum acceptable data loss (how old
   the last recoverable backup can be).

3. **Governance or management: approving the cybersecurity budget and risk
   appetite?**
   → Governance (the board). Implementing the controls it funds is management.

4. **Why is the backup *restore test* more important than the backup itself?**
   → A backup you have never restored may not work; only a verified restore proves
   you actually have disaster recovery — as Maersk's near-miss showed.

## Glossary

- **Governance vs management** — directing and assuring vs executing.
- **NIST CSF GOVERN / ISO 27001 / ISO 22301** — governance function / ISMS
  standard / business-continuity standard.
- **Three lines model** — operations / risk & compliance / internal audit.
- **Policy hierarchy** — Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.
- **MTTD / MTTR** — mean time to detect / respond.
- **BIA / RTO / RPO** — business-impact analysis / max downtime / max data loss.
- **Decision log** — timestamped record of crisis decisions and rationale.
