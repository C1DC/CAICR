# Module 5 — Cyber Fraud, BEC & Mobile Money Security

**Day 3 · Morning Session · ~4 hours (fraud, email forensics & payment security) · Hands-on lab: `labctl start m5`**

The most expensive cyberattacks in Ghana — and among the most expensive on earth
— do not break encryption, bypass a firewall, or drop any malware. They break
**trust**. They convince a real, competent, well-meaning person to send money to
the wrong account, and they do it so plausibly that the victim never suspects
until the funds are gone. This module teaches you to prove that fraud from the
evidence the attacker *cannot* hide, then to design the controls that stop whole
categories of it.

It covers **three connected domains**:

- **Email forensics** — the attacker's favourite delivery vehicle. You become an
  investigator: reading full headers, tracing a message to the server that truly
  sent it, exposing SPF/DKIM/DMARC failures with real tools, analysing the links,
  QR codes and attachments safely, and recovering the metadata that proves
  premeditation.
- **Business Email Compromise (BEC)** — the fraud those emails carry, why it works
  on competent people, and the one process control that defeats the whole category.
- **Payment & fintech security** — where the money actually moves: mobile money,
  card and payment-API fraud, PCI-DSS, 3-D Secure and tokenization, transaction-
  fraud detection and AML, and the "follow-the-money" race. *(For the fintech
  practitioners in the room, §6–§8 are written for you.)*

The two registers run throughout. The **conceptual** half explains *why* fraud
works and how the payment ecosystem is exposed; the **practical** half puts the
tools in your hands and gives you **easy, do-it-yourself exercises** (§10) on top
of the graded lab.

::: warning | A short ethics note
Email and payment forensics are defensive skills, but a few techniques here can be
misused. Only analyse mail **sent to you or your organisation**; only send test or
"spoof-check" mail (e.g. with `swaks`) against **your own domain**; never probe a
payment system you are not authorised to test. Ghana's **Act 1038** (cyber) and
**Act 987** (payment systems) both apply.
:::

## 1 · Fraud Is Deception, Not Malware

**Business Email Compromise (BEC)** is impersonation fraud conducted over email.
There is usually **no malware and no exploit**. The attacker studies a genuine
business relationship — often for weeks — then sends a message that *looks* like
it comes from a trusted party (a supplier, the Managing Director, a lawyer) asking
to change bank details or make an urgent payment. The victim is not hacked. They
are **deceived** into doing the attacker's work with their own hands and their own
authority.

Five classic plays recur:

- **CEO fraud** — "the MD" orders an urgent, confidential transfer.
- **Supplier impersonation** — a real supplier announces a "new bank account" mid-relationship.
- **Payroll diversion** — an "employee" asks HR to redirect their salary.
- **Attorney impersonation** — a "lawyer" invokes confidentiality and a deadline.
- **Gift-card fraud** — the low-value entry version, used to test a target.

---

## 2 · Why It Works — The Psychology, Named

Fraud is engineered against human decision-making, and two frameworks explain it
precisely.

**The Fraud Triangle** — fraud needs three things: *pressure*, *opportunity* and
*rationalisation*. BEC manufactures all three at once: **urgency** supplies the
pressure, a **weak approval process** supplies the opportunity, and a **plausible
story** ("the MD is in a board meeting and needs this done now") supplies the
rationalisation.

**Cialdini's principles of influence** — attackers weaponise **authority** ("this
is the MD"), **urgency/scarcity** ("the deadline is today"), **social proof**,
**liking** and **reciprocity**.

::: key | The BEC signature
When one message combines **authority + confidentiality + urgency + a nudge away
from phone contact** ("just email me, I'm in meetings"), your suspicion should
spike immediately. Those four together are the fingerprint of BEC — they exist to
switch off the victim's instinct to verify.
:::

The defender's structural advantage is this: **email carries its own evidence.**
The friendly `From:` name can say anything, but the headers record the true
delivery path and the authentication verdicts, and an attached document records
who made it and when. Reading that evidence is the whole craft.

## 3 · Email Header Forensics

The headers are read from the **bottom up** — the earliest `Received` line is the
first server that handled the message. Learn which field proves what:

| Header | What it proves |
|--------|----------------|
| `From:` | The *display* — attacker-controlled. **Trust it last.** |
| `Return-Path` / envelope sender | Where bounces really go — often the true look-alike domain. |
| `Reply-To` | Where *your reply* silently goes. A mismatch here is the classic trap. |
| `Received:` chain | The delivery path, bottom-up; the earliest hop shows the connecting IP — trace it with `whois`. |
| `Authentication-Results` | The verdicts: SPF, DKIM, DMARC pass/fail. |

The three authentication checks — the *receiving* side of the email-security
story you met in Module 1:

- **SPF** — was the sending server authorised to send for that domain? `fail` = no.
- **DKIM** — is there a valid cryptographic signature proving the message was not
  altered? `none` = unsigned.
- **DMARC** — the domain's published policy, and crucially **alignment**: does the
  authenticated domain match the visible `From:`? Alignment is what actually
  catches display-name spoofing.

```Terminal — trace a spoof from its own headers
# Pull the headers that matter
grep -iE '^(From|Return-Path|Reply-To|Received|Authentication)' invoice-change-request.eml

# Check the sending domain's SPF record, and who owns the connecting server
dig TXT zenith-supplies.com +short        # the look-alike domain's SPF
whois 45.137.112.63                        # ISP / country of the source IP
```

::: key | The genuine-vs-spoof contrast is the proof
This lab gives you **two** messages: the earlier *genuine* quote and the later
*spoofed* reply. On their own, a single "SPF fail" can have innocent causes. But
when the genuine email shows **SPF pass** from the real domain and the spoof shows
**SPF fail** from a hyphenated look-alike with a mismatched `Reply-To`, the
contrast is courtroom-grade. Always compare against a known-good baseline when you
have one.
:::

Watch for the **thread hijack**: a competent attacker reuses the real purchase-
order number and quotes the genuine email, so the fraud arrives inside what looks
like an ongoing, trusted conversation.

### 3.1 Reading the `Received` chain, line by line

The `Received:` headers are a stack: each mail server that handles the message
**prepends** its own line to the top. So you read them **bottom-up** — the
*lowest* line is the *first* hop, where the message entered the mail system, and
that line's `from ... ([IP])` is the **originating server**.

```Terminal — a Received chain, annotated (read bottom to top)
Received: from mx.yourbank.com.gh by imap.yourbank.com.gh ...     ← (3) your mailbox
Received: from mail.zenith-supplies.com (mail.zenith-supplies.com
          [45.137.112.63]) by mx.yourbank.com.gh ...              ← (2) your gateway accepted it
Received: from [192.168.8.14] (unknown [45.137.112.63])
          by mail.zenith-supplies.com ...                         ← (1) ORIGIN: whois this IP
Message-ID: <a93f@zenith-supplies.com>                            ← domain should match the sender
X-Mailer: PHPMailer 6.9                                           ← bulk-mail tooling, not Outlook
```

Three analyst moves on that block:

- **Trust from your boundary inward.** `Received` lines are only reliable *from
  your own receiving server backward* — the hops **your** infrastructure added.
  Everything **below** that first trustworthy hop was written by the sender's
  systems and can be **fabricated**, so never build a case on a lower `Received`
  line alone: corroborate the claimed origin with the **SPF/DKIM/DMARC results**
  (§3.2) and a `whois` on the connecting IP. The first hop your gateway
  (`mx.yourbank.com.gh`) recorded — and the IP it received *from* — is the real,
  trustworthy origin.
- **Message-ID** domain should match the sending domain; a mismatch (or a Gmail
  Message-ID on a "supplier" mail) is a tell.
- **X-headers** leak tooling: `X-Mailer: PHPMailer`, `X-Originating-IP`, or a
  consumer webmail stamp on a "corporate" message are all signals.

### 3.2 Reading `Authentication-Results` properly

Don't just note pass/fail — read the fields, because they name *which domain* was
checked:

```Terminal — one Authentication-Results header, decoded
Authentication-Results: mx.yourbank.com.gh;
  spf=fail (sender IP is not permitted) smtp.mailfrom=zenith-supplies.com;
  dkim=none;
  dmarc=fail (p=none) header.from=zenithsupplies.com.gh
```

- `smtp.mailfrom` is the **envelope** domain (the `Return-Path`); `header.from` is
  the **visible** `From:`. **DMARC alignment** requires the authenticated domain to
  *match* `header.from` — *relaxed* allows the same organisational domain, *strict*
  demands an exact match.
- Here SPF was checked against the look-alike `zenith-supplies.com` while the
  visible From is the real `zenithsupplies.com.gh` — they don't align, so **DMARC
  fails**. That misalignment is exactly how display-name spoofing is caught.
- But note `(p=none)` — the real domain publishes a *monitor-only* policy, so the
  forged mail was **delivered anyway**. Which is the perfect segue to the other
  half of the job: making sure *your* domain isn't the one being abused.

### 3.3 The email-forensics toolkit

Reading one header set by eye is the skill; these tools make you fast and
reliable on a real inbox.

**Get the raw message first.** You cannot analyse what your mail client prettifies.
In Gmail use **"Show original"**; in Outlook **"View source" / "Save as .eml"**; in
Thunderbird **"View Source"**. Convert an Outlook `.msg` with `msgconvert` (from
`libemail-outlook-message-perl`).

**Visual header analysers** turn the raw block into a delivery map in seconds:

| Tool | What it does |
|------|--------------|
| **Google Admin Toolbox — Messageheader** | paste headers → visual hop list + delays + SPF/DKIM/DMARC |
| **MXToolbox Email Header Analyzer** | same, plus blacklist/reputation lookups |
| **Microsoft MHA** (Message Header Analyzer) | detailed, Microsoft-mail-aware breakdown |

::: warning | Don't paste sensitive mail into third-party sites
A real customer's email may contain personal data. For anything sensitive, analyse
**locally** (below) rather than pasting headers into a public web tool — Act 843
(data protection) still applies to that evidence.
:::

**Parse it locally** — `grep` is fine for a quick look, but headers fold across
lines and are often encoded, so the Python standard library parses them reliably:

```Terminal — parse an .eml locally, no internet, no third party
grep -iE '^(From|Return-Path|Reply-To|Received|Authentication|Message-ID|X-)' msg.eml
# robust parse (handles folded/encoded headers):
python3 -c 'import email,sys; m=email.message_from_file(open(sys.argv[1])); [print(f"{k}: {v}") for k,v in m.items()]' msg.eml
```

**Check the sending infrastructure and the domain's records:**

```Terminal — who sent it, and what does the domain publish?
dig +short TXT example.com                     # SPF
dig +short TXT _dmarc.example.com              # DMARC policy
whois 45.137.112.63                            # ISP / country of the connecting IP
# reputation of that IP/domain — AbuseIPDB, GreyNoise, Talos (from the resources list)
```

**Test your *own* domain's defences** with `swaks` — try to spoof your own `From:`
to a test mailbox and confirm DMARC rejects it (only ever against a domain you own):

```Terminal — prove your anti-spoofing works (your own domain only)
swaks --to test@yourdomain.gh --from "CEO <ceo@yourdomain.gh>" \
      --server your-test-relay --header "Subject: spoof check"
# a hardened domain (p=reject) refuses or quarantines this; p=none lets it through
```

### 3.4 Links, QR codes & attachments — analyse before you click

The payload of a phishing mail is its **link** or **attachment**. Never click or
open — **extract and inspect**:

- **Defang** URLs in your notes so nobody clicks them by accident: `hxxps://bad[.]site`.
- **Inspect the real destination without visiting it:** paste into **urlscan.io**
  (sandboxed browser, screenshot, the domains it contacts) or **VirusTotal**;
  expand shorteners; check the domain on **Talos/urlscan**.
- **Decode obfuscation with CyberChef** — phishing links hide behind Base64, URL-
  encoding and **homoglyph/punycode** look-alikes (`раypal.com` with a Cyrillic
  *а*, or `xn--` punycode). CyberChef unmasks all of it offline.
- **Quishing (QR phishing):** a QR code *hides* its URL — decode it before trusting
  it. QR fraud is rising fast on fake invoices, posters and "scan to pay" stickers.
- **Attachments:** don't open — read metadata/macros (`exiftool`, `olevba`; §5) or
  detonate in a sandbox (Any.Run / Hybrid Analysis from the resources list).

```Terminal — inspect a link and a QR code safely
echo 'aHR0cHM6Ly9iYWQuc2l0ZS9sb2dpbg==' | base64 -d      # decode a Base64 link (or use CyberChef)
zbarimg suspicious-qr.png                                # reveal a QR's URL (apt install zbar-tools)
# then paste the revealed URL into urlscan.io / VirusTotal — do NOT open it in a browser
```

::: key | The homoglyph trap
`micros0ft.com`, `zenith‑supplies.com` (hyphen), `раypal.com` (Cyrillic а) and
`xn--pple-43d.com` (punycode "аpple") all render *almost* identically to the real
thing. Copy the domain into CyberChef or `python3 -c "print('<url>'.encode('idna'))"`
and the disguise falls away. Attackers rely on you reading fast.
:::

## 4 · Hardening Your Own Email — So You're Not the One Spoofed

Everything so far is the *receiver's* investigation. The flip side is the duty you
owe your own customers and partners: publish records so that **nobody can
successfully spoof your domain**. Three DNS records do it (you met them from the
receiving side in Module 1):

- **SPF** — a TXT record listing the servers allowed to send as you, ending in
  **`-all`** (hard fail). A softfail `~all` tells receivers "probably not us, but
  deliver anyway" — publish `-all` once you know all your senders.
- **DKIM** — sign every outbound message with a private key; publish the public
  key at a *selector* so receivers can verify nothing was altered.
- **DMARC** — a `_dmarc` TXT record that ties SPF+DKIM to your visible `From:` and
  tells receivers what to do with failures — and asks them to send you **aggregate
  reports** (`rua=`).

```Terminal — the records, and how to check them (tie-in to Module 1's dig)
dig TXT yourbank.com.gh           +short   # v=spf1 include:_spf.provider ... -all
dig TXT sel._domainkey.yourbank.com.gh +short   # v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIGf...
dig TXT _dmarc.yourbank.com.gh    +short   # v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@yourbank.com.gh
```

::: key | p=none protects no one — the goal is p=reject
Roll DMARC out in stages: **`p=none`** (monitor only — read the aggregate reports
to find your legitimate senders), then **`p=quarantine`** (fails go to spam), then
**`p=reject`** (fails are refused outright). Most organisations stall at `p=none`
and wonder why criminals still spoof them — *none* only watches. The GCB-style BEC
works partly because the impersonated supplier sat at `p=none`. Reaching
**`p=reject`** is the single highest-impact control to stop your brand being
weaponised against your own customers.
:::

## 5 · Attachment & Document Forensics

Attackers are meticulous about the email and careless about the file. Office
documents and PDFs silently record **metadata** — who authored them, in what
application, and *when* — and that metadata is often the attacker's undoing.
**exiftool** reads it from almost any file type; **pdfinfo** is a quick PDF-only
alternative.

```Terminal — exiftool exposes the attacker behind the attachment
exiftool remittance-advice.pdf
```
```Terminal — the fields that matter (illustrative; yours differ)
Author          : k1ngpin
Creator         : Microsoft® Word
Create Date     : 2026:09:28 23:41:07+07:00
Modify Date     : 2026:09:28 23:42:10+07:00
Producer        : Microsoft® Word
Title           : Remittance Advice
```

Three findings leap out, each independently damaging:

- **Author** — not "Zenith Supplies Finance" but a handle like `k1ngpin`. A
  genuine supplier's document does not carry a street-handle author name.
- **Timezone** — the `Create Date` ends in a **UTC offset** (e.g. `+07:00`) that
  is inconsistent with a Ghanaian supplier (Ghana is **+00:00**). The author sat
  in a very different part of the world.
- **Create Date vs send date** — this is the knockout.

::: key | Metadata proves premeditation
If the attachment's **Create Date (e.g. 2026-09-28)** is *days before* the email
was **sent (e.g. 2026-10-06)**, the fraud was **prepared in advance** — it was not
an impulsive error or a genuine mistake, but a planned operation. "Created before
sent" turns a civil dispute into evidence of deliberate, premeditated fraud. Check
this on every malicious document you handle.
:::

The defensive lesson cuts both ways: scrub metadata from documents *your*
organisation publishes (author names, internal paths and software versions are
reconnaissance gifts), and read it closely on anything suspicious you receive.

### 5.1 Beyond PDFs — Office documents and macros

`exiftool` reads **Office** files too (`.docx/.xlsx` are ZIP containers of XML —
`docProps/core.xml` holds author, `lastModifiedBy`, created/modified timestamps,
and a *revision count* that betrays a hastily cloned template). When the attachment
is a **macro-enabled** Office file (`.docm`, `.xlsm`) — the classic malware lure of
Module 6 — you triage the macro *without opening it* using `oletools`:

```Terminal — triage a macro-enabled document safely (never open it)
exiftool suspicious.docx                 # author, lastModifiedBy, timestamps
olevba suspicious.docm                   # dump VBA macros + flag auto-run/shell/download
```

`olevba` flags the dangerous verbs — `AutoOpen`, `Shell`, `powershell`,
`URLDownloadToFile` — that turn a document into a dropper. In this module the lure
is a PDF, but the instinct is universal: **read the file's metadata and code
before you trust its contents.**

## 6 · Mobile Money — Ghana's Fraud Battleground

Mobile money is how most Ghanaians bank, which makes it the country's busiest
fraud surface. The scams are overwhelmingly **social-engineering**, not hacking —
the same deception-not-malware principle as BEC, aimed at individuals and agents.

**The MoMo fraud taxonomy:**

- **SIM swap.** The fraudster persuades (or bribes) a mobile operator to port a
  victim's number to a new SIM, hijacking every SMS one-time code that "protects"
  the wallet — authentication bypassed **without touching a computer**.
- **Vishing / "your wallet will be blocked".** A scripted call ("I'm from MoMo
  support, confirm your PIN / approve the prompt I just sent to reactivate your
  account"). The victim approves the attacker's own transaction.
- **Prompt-approval fatigue.** The fraudster triggers a real "approve payment"
  prompt and talks the victim into accepting it.
- **Erroneous-transfer / reversal scam.** "I sent you money by mistake — please
  send it back." The original "credit" was itself fraudulent or reversed.
- **Phishing portals & fake agents.** Links to a cloned MoMo page, or a complicit
  agent processing unauthorised cash-outs.

**The ecosystem and why it's exposed.** Ghana's mobile money runs under the **Bank
of Ghana** and the **Payment Systems and Services Act 2019 (Act 987)**, cleared
through **GhIPSS**, across the telcos (MTN MoMo, Telecel, AT). Fraud is enabled at
the seams: weak **agent KYC**, SIM-registration gaps, and — technically — the
legacy telecom signalling protocol **SS7**, which has well-documented weaknesses
that let a motivated attacker intercept SMS OTPs across networks. That is the
deeper reason **SMS OTP is fundamentally weak**.

::: key | The controls that actually hold
For individuals: a strong unique PIN, device/SIM **binding**, and treating any
unsolicited call or prompt as hostile. For institutions and agents: **transaction
and daily limits**, **velocity/anomaly detection** (a sudden large transfer, a new
device, an odd location, rapid dispersal), **in-app out-of-band approval** rather
than SMS, and — on a business wallet — **dual authorisation**. Never trust a single
SMS OTP: SIM swap and SS7 defeat it entirely.
:::

## 7 · Payment Systems & Fintech Security

Mobile money is one rail; a fintech touches several — cards, bank transfers,
instant payments and the **APIs** that glue them together. Each is a distinct
fraud and attack surface, and knowing how the rails work is what lets you place
the right control. *(This section is written for the payment and fintech
practitioners; everyone benefits from knowing where the money actually moves.)*

### 7.1 The rails, and where fraud enters

| Rail | How it works | Common fraud | Key control |
|------|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| **Cards** (Visa/Mastercard/gh-link) | issuer ↔ acquirer ↔ network authorise a PAN | stolen card data, **card-not-present (CNP)**, skimming/POS, carding/BIN attacks, chargeback abuse | PCI-DSS, tokenization, **3-D Secure** |
| **Instant transfer** (GhIPSS **GIP**, GhanaPay) | real-time account-to-account | **APP fraud** (victim *tricked* into paying — BEC's cousin), mule networks | confirmation-of-payee, limits, out-of-band verify |
| **Mobile money** | wallet ↔ wallet via telco/GhIPSS | SIM swap, vishing, reversal (see §6) | device binding, in-app OOB approval |
| **Payment APIs / aggregators** (Paystack/Flutterwave/Hubtel-style) | keys + webhooks between merchant and PSP | **leaked API keys**, **webhook spoofing/replay**, double-spend | signed/verified webhooks, idempotency, mTLS |

::: key | Two fraud families need two different defences
**Technical fraud** steals data or breaks the system (card data theft, API abuse) —
defeated by PCI-DSS, tokenization, signed APIs and monitoring. **Authorised-push-
payment (APP) fraud** tricks the *legitimate* customer into paying — exactly like
BEC — and no amount of encryption stops it. APP fraud is defeated by the *same*
out-of-band verification, confirmation-of-payee and transaction friction that stop
BEC. A fintech must run **both** defences.
:::

### 7.2 PCI-DSS and protecting card data

If you **store, process or transmit** cardholder data you are in scope for
**PCI-DSS** (the card-industry security standard). The whole strategy is to
**shrink where the card number (PAN) can live**:

- **Tokenization** — replace the PAN with a meaningless token so your systems never
  hold real card numbers (massively shrinks PCI scope).
- **Encryption in transit and at rest**; never store the **CVV** after authorisation.
- **3-D Secure (EMV 3DS) / "Verified by Visa"** — a step-up challenge for CNP
  transactions, part of **Strong Customer Authentication (SCA)**.
- **Segment the cardholder-data environment (CDE)** (Module 3), control and log all
  access, and run the quarterly **ASV vulnerability scans** and pen tests (Module 4).

### 7.3 Payment-API & open-banking security

Modern fintech *is* APIs, and the **OWASP API Security Top 10** is its threat list.
The number-one risk, **Broken Object-Level Authorization (BOLA)**, is the classic
fintech bug: user A reads or pays from user B's account by changing an `id` in the
request (the same access-control flaw you met in the Juice Shop arena). Core
controls:

- **API keys/secrets** never in the client app or git; rotate them; least privilege.
- **Sign and verify webhooks** (HMAC) and use **idempotency keys** so a replayed or
  duplicated "payment succeeded" callback can't double-credit an account.
- **mTLS** between services, strict **rate limiting**, and authorise **every object
  access** server-side (fix BOLA) — never trust an ID from the client.
- **Open banking:** OAuth2 consent with **narrow scopes**; over-broad consent is the risk.

**The Ghana reporting chain** — in order, and in parallel where you can:

1. **Your bank's fraud desk** — immediately, to freeze and recall.
2. **The receiving bank / telco** — to freeze the mule account or wallet.
3. **The Cyber Security Authority / CERT-GH** — incident reporting under the
   **Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038)**.
4. **Bank of Ghana** — for a regulated institution's reporting obligations.
5. **Law enforcement** — the **Ghana Police Cybercrime Unit** and/or the
   **Economic and Organised Crime Office (EOCO)** for criminal investigation.

On the defender's side, banks catch mule activity with **velocity and anomaly
rules** — a dormant account suddenly receiving a large inflow and dispersing it in
minutes is the mule signature, and a good fraud-ops team alerts on exactly that.

## 9 · The Control That Stops BEC

One process defeats the *entire* category, and it costs nothing but discipline:

**Dual control with out-of-band verification.** No single person both initiates
*and* approves a payment, and **any change to bank details is confirmed by calling
the counterparty on a previously known number** — never the number in the email,
and never by replying to the email (the attacker set `Reply-To`). One phone call,
to a number you already had on file, stops BEC dead.

BEC maps to MITRE ATT&CK **T1566** (Phishing); the impersonation element maps to
**T1656** (Impersonation).

## 10 · Hands-On Practicals — Do These Yourself

These are **easy, safe, self-contained** exercises you can run on your own machine
and your own data — no lab targets required. Do them in order; they rehearse every
skill the graded lab (§12) then tests. **All of them stay inside the law:** you
analyse mail *you* received, read *public* DNS records, and inspect *your own*
files.

::: key | Practical 1 — dissect a real email from your own inbox (10 min)
Open any email **you** received → **"Show original" / "View source"**. Find the
`From:`, `Return-Path` and `Reply-To` — do they agree? Read the `Received:` chain
**bottom-up** to the first hop and `whois` that IP. Read `Authentication-Results`:
did **SPF/DKIM/DMARC** pass? *Win condition:* you can state the true origin and the
auth verdict for a real message.
:::

::: key | Practical 2 — audit a domain's anti-spoofing (5 min)
Pick a domain you use (your bank, your employer). Check what it publishes:
```Terminal
dig +short TXT yourbank.com.gh              # SPF — does it end in -all?
dig +short TXT _dmarc.yourbank.com.gh       # DMARC — p=none, quarantine, or reject?
```
*Win condition:* say whether that domain could be spoofed against its own
customers today (anything at `p=none` can be). Cross-check on **MXToolbox**.
:::

::: key | Practical 3 — the metadata you leak (5 min)
Run `exiftool` on a document **you** created:
```Terminal
exiftool my-report.pdf        # your author name, software, create/modify times
exiftool -all= -o clean.pdf my-report.pdf   # then scrub it for publishing
```
*Win condition:* you see what your own files reveal — and know to strip it before
publishing. This is the exact field that convicts the attacker in the lab.
:::

::: key | Practical 4 — unmask an obfuscated / look-alike link (10 min)
Decode this (safely, offline) and spot why it is dangerous:
```Terminal
echo 'aHR0cHM6Ly9zZWN1cmUtcGF5cGE1LmNvbS9sb2dpbg==' | base64 -d
```
Then compare `paypal.com`, `paypa1.com` and `раypal.com` (that last *а* is
Cyrillic). Paste a punycode domain into **CyberChef** or
`python3 -c "print('раypal.com'.encode('idna'))"`. *Win condition:* you can explain
the homoglyph/punycode trap to a colleague.
:::

::: key | Practical 5 — reveal a QR code's destination (5 min)
QR codes hide their URL. Decode one before trusting it:
```Terminal
zbarimg a-qr-code.png         # apt install zbar-tools  — prints the real URL
```
Then paste that URL into **urlscan.io** (never your browser). *Win condition:* you
never "scan to pay" again without checking where it points.
:::

::: key | Practical 6 — the Spot-the-BEC drill (10 min)
Take three messages (your spam folder works) and score each against the **BEC
signature**: authority + confidentiality + urgency + a nudge away from the phone.
For any that match, name the **one control** that stops it (out-of-band callback on
a known number). *Win condition:* you recognise the pattern in seconds.
:::

::: key | Practical 7 (fintech) — write one fraud-detection rule (10 min)
In plain words, write a **transaction-monitoring rule** that would catch a money
mule: e.g. *"a < 90-day-old account that receives > GHS 10,000 and disperses > 80%
of it within 30 minutes → hold + step-up + AML review."* State the signals it uses
(age, velocity, amount, dispersal) and the action. *Win condition:* a rule a
fraud-ops team could actually implement (§7.4).
:::

Optional, only on a domain you own: send yourself a spoofed-`From:` with **`swaks`**
(§3.3) and watch whether your DMARC policy rejects it — the most convincing proof
that `p=reject` works.

## 11 · Scenarios — The Costliest Con in the World

::: ghana | Ghana & West Africa
A **GCB Bank BEC (~GHS 2.3M)** ran the full playbook: reconnaissance of the
procurement team, a look-alike supplier domain registered weeks in advance, a
bank-detail-change email, and manufactured urgency to push it through. Every
technical signal was in the headers — SPF fail, look-alike domain, mismatched
`Reply-To` — but the decisive failure was **process**: no dual-control callback on
a known number. **One phone call would have stopped it.** The 2022–2023 SIM-swap
wave separately showed how SMS-OTP "security" collapses the moment the number
itself is stolen.
:::

::: case | Google & Facebook, 2013–2015 — ~US$100 million
Evaldas Rimašauskas impersonated a real hardware supplier (Quanta) with look-alike
invoices, contracts and emails. Two of the most technically sophisticated
companies on earth paid, because the **process** failed, not the technology. If
Google and Facebook can be taken for US$100M by a man with forged invoices, no
organisation is "too technical" to fall — and the fix is process, not spend.
:::

::: case | Ubiquiti (2015, ~US$46.7M) and Bangladesh Bank (2016, ~US$81M)
Ubiquiti's finance staff were deceived by CEO-fraud emails into wiring funds to
attacker accounts. Bangladesh Bank lost US$81M to fraudulent SWIFT transfers after
a long intrusion — fraud at the scale of a nation's reserves. Different scales,
identical lesson: **BEC beats technology because it targets process and people.**
:::

## 12 · The Worked Investigation (what the lab asks of you)

`labctl start m5` is a **file-based** lab. In the lab's `assets/` you have the
earlier **genuine** supplier quote, the later **spoofed** reply, and the malicious
**PDF attachment**. Work the evidence:

1. **Headers.** In the spoof, find the **envelope sender** (`Return-Path`), the
   true **source IP** of the sending server, and the **SPF result** — then contrast
   it with the genuine email's **SPF pass**. Identify the **look-alike domain** and
   the **real supplier domain** it impersonates, the reused **PO number** (the
   thread hijack), and the **fraudulent account number** in the body.
2. **Attachment.** Run **exiftool** on the PDF to recover the **author handle**,
   the inconsistent **timezone offset**, and whether it was **created before the
   email was sent** (premeditation).
3. **Conclude.** Name the **payment control** that stops it and the **ATT&CK**
   technique.

::: warning | Your values are unique
The source IP, the look-alike domain, the fraudulent account number, the PDF
author handle and its timezone offset are **randomised per student**. The examples
in this lesson (`zenith-supplies.com`, `k1ngpin`, `+07:00`) are illustrative —
read *your* evidence. `labctl verify m5` grades against your personal answer key.
:::

Then write the **Fraud Investigation Brief** (`labctl worksheet m5`): the
summary, the header findings, the evidence timeline, the fraudulent payment
details for a bank recall, the control failures, and the process fix.

## Common mistakes

::: warning | What trips students up
- **Trusting the `From:` display name** — the single least reliable field in the message.
- **Analysing the prettified view.** Always get the **raw** message ("Show
  original" / `.eml`) — the client hides the headers that matter.
- **Pasting sensitive mail into public header-analyser sites.** Use local tools
  (`grep`, the Python `email` parser) for anything with personal data (Act 843).
- **Clicking the link or opening the attachment to "check".** Defang, then inspect
  via urlscan/VirusTotal/CyberChef and `exiftool`/`olevba` — never open it.
- **Reading a domain fast.** Homoglyphs and punycode (`раypal.com`, `xn--…`) look
  identical — decode them.
- **Replying to verify.** The attacker set `Reply-To`; your "are you sure?" reaches
  *them*, and they reply "yes, hurry". Verify on an **independently known** number.
- **Believing SMS OTP is strong.** SIM swap and SS7 defeat it entirely.
- **Treating all payment fraud as technical.** **APP fraud** tricks the real
  customer — encryption won't help; it needs out-of-band verification like BEC.
- **Storing card data you don't need.** Every PAN/CVV you keep is PCI scope and
  liability — tokenize and shrink it.
- **Ignoring the attachment, or reporting the spoof alone.** The metadata is often
  the confession, and the genuine-vs-spoof *contrast* is what makes it unarguable.
:::

## Key takeaways

::: key | Carry these out of Module 5
**Email forensics & BEC**
- BEC is **deception, not malware** — it weaponises the **Fraud Triangle** and
  **Cialdini's** authority + urgency + confidentiality.
- **Headers hold the truth**: `Return-Path`, the `Received` chain (read **bottom-up**
  to the origin IP), `Authentication-Results` by field (`smtp.mailfrom` vs
  `header.from` = DMARC alignment) — never the `From:` display. Compare against a
  known-good baseline.
- **Use the toolkit:** raw message → Message Header Analyzer / MXToolbox or a local
  `email`-module parse → `dig`/`whois` the records and IP → `swaks` to test *your
  own* domain.
- **Inspect links/QR/attachments before you trust them:** defang, urlscan/VirusTotal,
  **CyberChef** for obfuscation and homoglyph/punycode, `zbarimg` for QR,
  `exiftool`/`olevba` for files — never click or open.
- **Harden your own domain:** SPF `-all`, DKIM, DMARC to **`p=reject`** (`p=none`
  protects no one); attachment metadata often proves **premeditation**.

**Payment & fintech**
- Two fraud families, two defences: **technical fraud** (card/API theft) → PCI-DSS,
  **tokenization**, signed APIs, monitoring; **APP fraud** (the customer tricked) →
  out-of-band verification, like BEC.
- **PCI-DSS**: shrink where the PAN lives (tokenize, never store CVV, segment the
  CDE, scan). **APIs**: fix **BOLA**, sign/verify webhooks, idempotency keys, rotate
  secrets. **Risk-score every transaction** in real time (velocity/device/geo/new
  payee) with step-up; run **AML/KYC/mule detection** and report to the **FIC**.
- **Mobile money** fraud is social-engineering: **SIM swap and SS7 defeat SMS OTP**;
  hold with limits, anomaly detection, in-app OOB approval and dual authorisation.

**Respond**
- **Follow the money fast** — the **golden hour**: freeze/recall before cash-out,
  work the reporting chain (bank → receiving bank/telco → CSA/CERT-GH → BoG →
  Police Cybercrime/EOCO).
- **Dual control + out-of-band callback on a known number** stops BEC. ATT&CK:
  **T1566 / T1656**.
:::

## Check yourself

1. **`From:` shows the real supplier, but `Reply-To` is a different, hyphenated
   domain. What's happening?**
   → A spoof — the display is forged to earn trust while replies route silently to
   the attacker's look-alike domain.

2. **The genuine email shows `spf=pass`; the new "supplier" email shows
   `spf=fail; dkim=none; dmarc=fail`. Verdict, and why is the pair so powerful?**
   → The second is forged. The *contrast* with a known-good baseline removes any
   innocent explanation — it is courtroom-grade proof.

3. **exiftool shows the attachment's Create Date is 8 days before the email was
   sent, with a `+07:00` timezone. What two things does this prove?**
   → **Premeditation** (prepared in advance, not an accident) and a **foreign
   author** (a Ghanaian supplier is +00:00).

4. **A finance officer gets an urgent supplier request to change bank details. The
   single step that stops BEC?**
   → Call the supplier on a *previously known* number (out-of-band, dual control) —
   never reply, never use the email's number.

5. **Google and Facebook lost ~US$100M to BEC. What does that prove about the
   defence?**
   → The weakness is **process and people**, not technology, so the fix is a
   process control (dual control + callback), not more software.

6. **Your own domain sits at DMARC `p=none`. Are your customers protected from
   being phished in your name? What do you change?**
   → No — `p=none` only *monitors*; forged mail is still delivered. Read the
   aggregate reports to confirm your legitimate senders, then move to
   `p=quarantine` and finally **`p=reject`**.

7. **A finance officer realises an hour ago they paid a fraudulent "supplier"
   account. First three actions, in order?**
   → (1) Call **your bank's fraud desk** to freeze/recall; (2) have them contact the
   **receiving bank/telco** to freeze that account; (3) report to **CSA/CERT-GH**
   (and preserve the emails/headers). It's the **golden hour** — speed decides
   recovery.

8. **A "supplier" email links to `zenith‑supplies.com`. Why can't you trust your
   eyes, and how do you check?**
   → Homoglyph/punycode look-alikes render almost identically to the real domain.
   Decode it (CyberChef or `.encode('idna')`) and inspect in urlscan.io — never open
   it in a browser.

9. **A customer insists *they* authorised the transfer, yet it was fraud. Which
   fraud family is this, and why won't PCI-DSS or encryption help?**
   → **Authorised-push-payment (APP) fraud** — the real customer was *tricked*, so
   the transaction is technically legitimate. Only out-of-band verification,
   confirmation-of-payee and transaction friction stop it (the same fix as BEC).

10. **A fintech's API lets user A fetch user B's transactions by changing an `id`.
    Name the flaw and the fix.**
    → **BOLA** (Broken Object-Level Authorization, OWASP API #1). Authorise every
    object access **server-side** against the authenticated user — never trust an ID
    from the client.

11. **Why is tokenization the centre of a card-data security strategy?**
    → It replaces the real PAN with a meaningless token, so your systems never hold
    card numbers — which shrinks PCI-DSS scope and means a breach of your database
    leaks nothing usable.

## Glossary

- **BEC** — Business Email Compromise; fraud by email impersonation.
- **Fraud Triangle** — pressure + opportunity + rationalisation.
- **Cialdini's principles** — authority, urgency/scarcity, social proof, liking, reciprocity.
- **Return-Path / Received / Reply-To** — headers revealing true origin, path and reply destination.
- **Received chain** — the stack of handling servers; read bottom-up to the originating IP.
- **Authentication-Results / smtp.mailfrom / header.from** — the verdicts header / the envelope domain / the visible From; DMARC alignment compares them.
- **SPF / DKIM / DMARC / alignment** — authorised sender / signature / policy / From-domain match.
- **DMARC policy (p=none/quarantine/reject)** — monitor only / send fails to spam / refuse fails outright.
- **exiftool / pdfinfo / olevba** — read file metadata / PDF info / dump & flag Office macros.
- **Metadata / premeditation** — data about the file (author, dates, timezone) / proof the fraud was prepared in advance.
- **Thread hijack** — reusing a real PO number / quoting a genuine email to ride an existing trust.
- **SIM swap / SS7** — number hijack / the legacy telecom signalling it abuses.
- **Money mule / layering / golden hour** — an account used to move stolen funds / splitting funds to obscure them / the brief window to freeze before cash-out.
- **GhIPSS / Act 987 / CERT-GH / EOCO** — Ghana's payments switch / the Payment Systems Act / the national CERT / the Economic & Organised Crime Office.
- **Dual control / out-of-band** — split initiate-and-approve / verify on a separate trusted channel.
- **Message Header Analyzer / MXToolbox** — tools that turn raw headers into a visual delivery path + SPF/DKIM/DMARC verdicts.
- **swaks** — the Swiss-army SMTP tool; here, to test your *own* domain's anti-spoofing.
- **Defang / homoglyph / punycode (IDN)** — rendering a URL unclickable in notes / look-alike characters / `xn--` encoding of international domains used to disguise look-alikes.
- **urlscan.io / CyberChef / zbarimg** — sandboxed URL scanner / offline decode-everything tool / QR-code decoder.
- **Quishing** — phishing via a QR code that hides its destination URL.
- **APP fraud** — Authorised Push Payment fraud: the real customer is tricked into paying (needs BEC-style controls, not encryption).
- **PCI-DSS / PAN / CVV / tokenization** — the card-data security standard / the card number / the card security code (never store it) / replacing the PAN with a meaningless token to shrink scope.
- **3-D Secure (EMV 3DS) / SCA** — the step-up challenge for card-not-present payments / Strong Customer Authentication.
- **BOLA** — Broken Object-Level Authorization; the #1 OWASP API risk — accessing another user's object by changing an ID.
- **Webhook signing / idempotency key** — HMAC-verifying a callback's authenticity / a token that makes a replayed payment callback harmless.
- **Transaction monitoring / step-up / ATO** — real-time fraud scoring / an extra challenge on risky transactions / account takeover.
- **AML/CFT / KYC / CDD / FIC / Act 1044** — anti-money-laundering & counter-financing of terrorism / know-your-customer / customer due diligence / Ghana's Financial Intelligence Centre / the Anti-Money Laundering Act.
- **GIP / GhanaPay / gh-link** — GhIPSS Instant Pay / the bank-backed wallet / the national card scheme.
