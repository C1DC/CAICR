## The big picture

**Business Email Compromise (BEC)** is fraud by impersonation over email. There's
usually no malware. The attacker studies a real business relationship, then sends
a message that *looks* like it's from a trusted party — a supplier, the CEO, a
lawyer — asking to change bank details or make an urgent payment. The victim
isn't hacked; they're *deceived* into doing the attacker's work for them.

The five classic BEC plays:

- **CEO fraud** — "the MD" urgently orders a transfer.
- **Supplier impersonation** — a real supplier's "new bank account".
- **Payroll diversion** — an employee's salary redirected.
- **Attorney impersonation** — a "lawyer" pressing confidentiality and speed.
- **Gift-card fraud** — the low-value entry version.

The attacker's levers are psychological: **urgency, authority, confidentiality,
and a plausible reason email is better than a call.** Spot those four together
and your suspicion should spike.

The defender's power is that **email carries its own evidence**. The friendly
`From:` name can say anything, but the **headers** record the true delivery path
and the authentication results. Reading them is the whole game.

## An analogy

A paper letter can be signed "The Managing Director" in any handwriting. But the
*envelope* tells a different story: the postmark (where it was actually posted),
the return address, the stamps. BEC is a forged signature on fancy letterhead.
Header forensics is examining the envelope and postmark — and discovering the
"MD's" letter was posted from a different country by someone who registered a
near-identical company name last week.

## How it actually works

The key header fields, and what each proves:

- **`From:`** — the *display*. Attacker-controlled. Trust it last.
- **`Return-Path` / envelope sender** — where bounces really go. Often reveals
  the true (look-alike) domain.
- **`Reply-To`** — where *your reply* silently goes. A mismatch here is a classic
  trap: you "reply to the MD" and it lands with the attacker.
- **`Received:` chain** — the delivery path, built bottom-up. The *earliest*
  (lowest) hop shows the IP that actually connected — trace it with `whois`.
- **`Authentication-Results`** — the verdicts: **SPF**, **DKIM**, **DMARC**
  pass/fail.

The three authentication checks (same ones from Module 1, now seen from the
receiving side):

- **SPF** — was the sending server allowed to send for that domain? `fail` means
  no.
- **DKIM** — is there a valid signature proving the message wasn't altered?
  `none` means unsigned.
- **DMARC** — the policy. `fail` with a weak or absent policy means the forged
  mail is delivered anyway.

The other half of the module is **Ghana-specific mobile-money fraud**:

- **SIM swap** — the fraudster convinces the network operator to move a victim's
  number to a new SIM, instantly hijacking the SMS OTPs that "protect" the MoMo
  wallet. Authentication bypassed without touching a computer.
- **Agent impersonation & vishing** — "Your MoMo will be blocked — confirm your
  PIN now." Pure social engineering, scripted and rehearsed.

And the single control that stops BEC dead: **dual control with out-of-band
verification.** No one person both initiates *and* approves a payment, and any
change to bank details is confirmed by calling the counterparty on a
**previously known** number — never the number in the email. BEC maps to ATT&CK
**T1566** (Phishing); impersonation to **T1656**.

## Ghana in focus

The **GCB Bank BEC** (~GHS 2.3 million) ran the full playbook: reconnaissance of
the procurement team, a look-alike supplier domain registered weeks early, a
compromised-looking email changing bank details, and urgency to push the payment
through. Every technical control to detect it existed in the headers — SPF fail,
look-alike domain, mismatched Reply-To. But the decisive failure was a *process*
one: no dual-control callback on a known number. One phone call to the real
supplier would have stopped it. Separately, the **2022–2023 SIM-swap wave**
showed how SMS-OTP "security" collapses the moment the number itself is stolen —
which is why enterprise MoMo needs limits, alerts and dual authorisation, not
just an OTP.

## Why it matters

- BEC is routinely the **costliest** category of cybercrime for institutions —
  and the cheapest to prevent, because the fix is process, not technology.
- Header forensics lets you prove fraud *before* the money moves, turning a
  suspicious feeling into evidence anyone can act on.
- The same `dig`/header skills protect your *own* domain from being the one
  spoofed.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the `From:` name.** It is the least reliable field in the message.
- **Replying to verify.** The attacker set `Reply-To` — your "are you sure?"
  reaches *them*, who say "yes, hurry".
- **Verifying on the number in the email.** Always use an independently known
  number.
- **Thinking SMS OTP is strong.** SIM swap defeats it entirely.

## Key takeaways

- BEC is **deception, not malware** — it exploits urgency, authority and trust.
- **Headers hold the truth**: Return-Path, Received chain, Authentication-Results
  — not the `From:` display.
- **SIM swap** defeats SMS OTP; enterprise MoMo needs limits + dual authorisation.
- **Dual control + out-of-band callback on a known number** stops BEC.
- ATT&CK: **T1566** (Phishing), **T1656** (Impersonation).

## Check yourself

1. **The `From:` shows the real supplier, but `Reply-To` is a different,
   hyphenated domain. What's happening?**
   → A spoof: the display is forged to earn trust, while replies are silently
   routed to the attacker's look-alike domain.

2. **`Authentication-Results` shows `spf=fail; dkim=none; dmarc=fail`. Verdict?**
   → The message is almost certainly forged — it failed every authentication
   check. Treat it as fraudulent.

3. **A finance officer gets an urgent supplier request to change bank details.
   What's the one step that stops BEC?**
   → Call the supplier on a *previously known* number to confirm — out-of-band,
   dual-control verification. Never reply or use the email's number.

4. **Why doesn't an SMS OTP protect a MoMo wallet against SIM swap?**
   → Because the attacker has taken over the *number itself*, so the OTP is
   delivered straight to them.

## Glossary

- **BEC** — Business Email Compromise; fraud by email impersonation.
- **Return-Path / Received / Reply-To** — headers revealing true origin, path and
  reply destination.
- **SPF / DKIM / DMARC** — email authentication: authorised sender / signature /
  policy.
- **SIM swap** — hijacking a phone number to a new SIM to steal OTPs.
- **Dual control** — separation of initiate and approve for payments.
- **Out-of-band verification** — confirming via a different, trusted channel.
