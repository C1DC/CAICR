The most expensive cyberattacks in Ghana — and among the most expensive on earth
— do not break encryption. They break **trust**: they convince a real, competent
person to send money to the wrong account. This module teaches you to prove that
fraud from the evidence the attacker cannot hide, and to design the process that
stops it.

By the end you will be able to:

- explain the psychology of social engineering and the Ghana-specific MoMo

## 1. The big picture: fraud is deception, not malware

**Business Email Compromise (BEC)** is impersonation fraud over email. There is
usually no malware and no exploit. The attacker studies a real business
relationship, then sends a message that *looks* like it comes from a trusted
party — a supplier, the CEO, a lawyer — asking to change bank details or make an
urgent payment. The victim is not hacked; they are *deceived* into doing the
attacker's work.

The five classic plays: **CEO fraud** ("the MD" orders a transfer), **supplier
impersonation** (a real supplier's "new account"), **payroll diversion**,
**attorney impersonation** (confidentiality + speed), and **gift-card fraud**
(the low-value entry version).

## 2. Why it works: the psychology, named

Fraud is engineered against human decision-making. Two frameworks explain it:

- **The Fraud Triangle** — fraud needs *pressure*, *opportunity* and
  *rationalisation*. BEC manufactures all three: urgency (pressure), a weak
  approval process (opportunity), and a plausible story (rationalisation).
- **Cialdini's principles of influence** — attackers weaponise **authority**
  ("this is the MD"), **urgency/scarcity** ("the deadline is today"), **social
  proof**, **liking** and **reciprocity**. When you see *authority +
  confidentiality + urgency + "email is easier than a call"* in one message, your
  suspicion should spike. Those four together are the signature of BEC.

The defender's advantage: **email carries its own evidence.** The friendly
`From:` name can say anything, but the headers record the true delivery path and
the authentication verdicts. Reading them is the whole game.

## 3. How header forensics actually works

| Header | What it proves |
|--------|----------------|
| `From:` | The *display* — attacker-controlled. Trust it last. |
| `Return-Path` / envelope sender | Where bounces really go — often the true look-alike domain. |
| `Reply-To` | Where *your reply* silently goes. A mismatch here is a classic trap. |
| `Received:` chain | The delivery path, built bottom-up; the earliest hop shows the IP that connected — trace it with `whois`. |
| `Authentication-Results` | The verdicts: SPF, DKIM, DMARC pass/fail. |

The three authentication checks (the receiving side of Module 1):

- **SPF** — was the sending server authorised for that domain? `fail` = no.
- **DKIM** — is there a valid signature proving the message wasn't altered?
  `none` = unsigned.
- **DMARC** — the published policy. A `fail` against a weak/absent policy means the
  forged mail is delivered anyway, and **alignment** (the authenticated domain
  matching the visible `From:`) is what actually catches display-name spoofing.

```
grep -iE '^(From|Return-Path|Reply-To|Received|Authentication)' message.eml
dig TXT lookalike-domain.com        # SPF of the sending domain
whois 45.137.112.63                 # who owns the connecting server?
```

## 4. Mobile money — the Ghana-specific threat

- **SIM swap** — the fraudster convinces the mobile operator to move a victim's
  number to a new SIM, instantly hijacking the SMS one-time codes that "protect"
  the MoMo wallet. Authentication is bypassed without touching a computer. The
  deeper enabler is weak operator process and legacy telecom signalling (**SS7**),
  which has well-documented interception weaknesses.
- **Agent impersonation & vishing** — "Your MoMo will be blocked — confirm your
  PIN now." Pure, scripted social engineering.
- Enterprise MoMo controls: separate business wallets, daily limits, real-time
  alerts, and **dual authorisation** — never a single OTP.

## 5. The control that stops BEC

One process defeats the entire category: **dual control with out-of-band
verification.** No single person both initiates *and* approves a payment, and any
change to bank details is confirmed by calling the counterparty on a
**previously known** number — never the number in the email. BEC maps to ATT&CK
**T1566** (Phishing); impersonation to **T1656**.

## 6. Scenarios — the costliest con in the world

### Local (Ghana)

- **GCB Bank BEC (~GHS 2.3M)** ran the full playbook: reconnaissance of the
  procurement team, a look-alike supplier domain registered weeks early, a
  bank-detail-change email, and urgency to push it through. Every technical signal
  was in the headers (SPF fail, look-alike domain, mismatched Reply-To) — but the
  decisive failure was *process*: no dual-control callback on a known number. One
  phone call would have stopped it.
- The **2022–2023 SIM-swap wave** showed how SMS-OTP "security" collapses the
  moment the number itself is stolen.

### International (well-documented)

- **Google and Facebook, 2013–2015 (~US$100M).** Evaldas Rimasauskas impersonated
  a real hardware supplier (Quanta) with look-alike invoices and emails. Two of
  the most sophisticated technology companies on earth paid, because the *process*
  failed, not the technology.
- **Ubiquiti Networks, 2015 (~US$46.7M).** Finance staff were deceived by CEO-fraud
  emails into wiring funds to attacker-controlled accounts.
- **Bangladesh Bank, 2016 (~US$81M).** Fraudulent SWIFT transfers after a long
  intrusion — fraud at the scale of a nation's reserves.

If Google and Facebook can be taken for US$100M by a man with look-alike
invoices, the lesson is unmistakable: **BEC beats technology because it targets
process and people.** The fix is process.

## 7. Why it matters

- BEC is routinely the **costliest** category of cybercrime for institutions — and
  among the *cheapest* to prevent, because the fix is process, not spend.
- Header forensics lets you prove fraud *before* the money moves, turning a
  suspicion into evidence anyone can act on.
- The same `dig`/header skills protect your *own* domain from being the one
  spoofed.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Trusting the `From:` name** — the least reliable field in the message.
- **Replying to verify** — the attacker set `Reply-To`; your "are you sure?"
  reaches *them*, who say "yes, hurry".
- **Verifying on the number in the email** — always use an independently known
  number.
- **Believing SMS OTP is strong** — SIM swap defeats it entirely.

## Key takeaways

- BEC is **deception, not malware** — it weaponises the **fraud triangle** and
  **Cialdini's** authority + urgency + confidentiality.
- **Headers hold the truth**: Return-Path, Received chain, Authentication-Results
  — not the `From:` display.
- **SIM swap** defeats SMS OTP; enterprise MoMo needs limits + dual authorisation.
- **Dual control + out-of-band callback on a known number** stops BEC. ATT&CK:
  **T1566 / T1656**.

## Check yourself

1. **`From:` shows the real supplier, but `Reply-To` is a different, hyphenated
   domain. What's happening?**
   → A spoof: the display is forged to earn trust while replies route silently to
   the attacker's look-alike domain.

2. **`Authentication-Results: spf=fail; dkim=none; dmarc=fail`. Verdict?**
   → Almost certainly forged — it failed every authentication check. Treat as
   fraud.

3. **A finance officer gets an urgent supplier request to change bank details.
   The single step that stops BEC?**
   → Call the supplier on a *previously known* number (out-of-band, dual control).
   Never reply or use the email's number.

4. **Google and Facebook lost ~US$100M to BEC. What does that prove about the
   defence?**
   → That the weakness is *process and people*, not technology — so the fix is a
   process control (dual control + callback), not more software.

## Glossary

- **BEC** — Business Email Compromise; fraud by email impersonation.
- **Fraud triangle** — pressure + opportunity + rationalisation.
- **Cialdini's principles** — authority, urgency/scarcity, social proof, liking,
  reciprocity.
- **Return-Path / Received / Reply-To** — headers revealing true origin, path and
  reply destination.
- **SPF / DKIM / DMARC / alignment** — authorised sender / signature / policy /
  From-domain match.
- **SIM swap / SS7** — number hijack / the legacy telecom signalling it abuses.
- **Dual control / out-of-band** — split initiate-and-approve / verify on a
  separate trusted channel.
