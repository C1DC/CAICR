# Module 8 — Data Protection, Privacy & Cybersecurity Compliance

**Day 4 · Afternoon · Leadership module (no Kali lab — you build a compliance assessment)**

In Ghana, protecting personal data and reporting serious incidents is not
goodwill — it is the law, with a regulator, powers and penalties behind it. This
module teaches you which laws apply, what they actually require, how the global
regime (GDPR) shapes expectations, and what to do in the first hours of a breach
— including which questions you must not answer without a lawyer.

By the end you will be able to:
- apply the Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843) to real data handling;
- explain the Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038) and the CSA's powers;
- run the first 72 hours of a breach with the right triage decisions;
- know where Ghana's regime aligns with, and differs from, the global standard.

---

## 1. The big picture: two laws, one duty of care

- **Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843)** governs *personal data*, overseen by the
  **Data Protection Commission (DPC)**. If you hold data about identifiable people
  — customers, citizens, staff — it applies to you.
- **Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038)** governs *cybersecurity*. It created the
  **Cyber Security Authority (CSA)**, licenses cybersecurity providers, designates
  **Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)**, and sets incident-reporting
  duties. **CERT-GH** is the national coordination centre under the CSA.

Act 843 rests on data-protection **principles**: process data lawfully and for a
stated **purpose**; collect only what you need (**minimisation**); keep it
**accurate** and **secure**; keep it only as long as needed (**retention**); and
be **accountable** for all of it. Practical rule: *if you hold personal data in
Ghana, you are a data controller — register with the DPC, comply, protect.*

Two roles with different obligations:

- **Data controller** — decides *why and how* data is processed (your
  institution). Carries the primary legal liability.
- **Data processor** — processes it *on the controller's behalf* (e.g. a cloud
  vendor). **Outsourcing the processing does not outsource your accountability.**

Some data is **sensitive** — health, biometrics, financial, criminal records —
and demands heightened protection.

## 2. The global benchmark: GDPR

Even when it does not directly bind you, the EU **GDPR** sets the expectations
clients, partners and regulators increasingly assume: a **72-hour** breach-
notification norm, data-subject rights (access, rectification, erasure),
**Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)** for high-risk processing, privacy
by design and by default, and very large fines (up to 4% of global turnover). Its
enforcement shows the stakes: **British Airways** and **Marriott** drew
multi-million-pound penalties after breaches of customer data. Knowing GDPR tells
you where the regulatory world is heading — and where Ghanaian practice is
expected to meet it.

## 3. How breach response actually works — the first 72 hours

A disciplined order, aligned with the technical work in Module 6:

1. **Contain first, investigate second** — stop active access (while preserving
   evidence — Module 6's "isolate, don't power off").
2. **Scope it** — what data, which systems, how many people, is the attacker still
   present?
3. **Triage notification** — scope and data sensitivity drive the obligation.
4. **Engage legal counsel** *before* any external communication.

Notification differs by breach type — a working mental grid:

| Breach type | Immediate action | Notification question |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Ransomware, systems encrypted only | Isolate, preserve, restore from clean backup | Was data *exfiltrated*? If yes, notification may apply |
| Ransomware + confirmed data theft | Isolate, preserve, engage counsel now | Notification likely — scope & timing need legal advice |
| Phishing / credential compromise | Reset creds, revoke sessions, investigate | What could that account reach? |
| BEC / financial fraud | Preserve email evidence, contact bank to recall | Financial recovery first; data exposure may be secondary |
| Cloud misconfiguration exposing data | Remove public access, then scope duration | Duration + sensitivity drive the obligation |

For **CII operators**, Act 1038 imposes **mandatory incident reporting** to the
CSA/CERT-GH. Because exact timelines and formats can change, treat them as a
matter to confirm with counsel and the current CSA directives — not to guess.

## 4. An analogy

Treat personal data as cash you hold *in trust* for other people. You don't own
it; you are a custodian. You keep only what you were given reason to hold, store
it in a safe, don't lend it out carelessly, return or destroy it when the reason
ends — and you can be audited on how you handled it. Hiring a security-van firm
(a processor) to move the cash does not end your responsibility for it; if they
lose it, *you* still answer to the owner.

## 5. Scenarios — exposure and consequence

### Local (Ghana)

- A Ghanaian hospital discovers a cloud repository of patient records left
  publicly accessible, with logs showing external downloads. Act 843 is squarely
  engaged: the data is *sensitive* (health); the hospital is the *controller* even
  though a vendor ran the cloud; and the download logs mean this is probable
  *exfiltration*, not mere exposure. The immediate steps — remove public access,
  preserve the logs, scope, and get counsel on DPC notification — are the fusion
  of Module 6's forensics and this module's law.

### International (well-documented)

- **Equifax, 2017.** Beyond the technical failure, the aftermath — regulatory
  settlements, consumer redress, executive departures — showed that a breach is a
  *legal and governance* event, not only a technical one.
- **British Airways & Marriott** GDPR penalties demonstrated that regulators now
  fine inadequate protection of personal data heavily, independent of the
  attacker's sophistication.

## 6. Why it matters

- Non-compliance carries real consequences — DPC enforcement, CSA action, and the
  reputational and financial hit of a public breach.
- Knowing your controller/processor status tells you *whose* responsibility a
  breach is (almost always still yours).
- A rehearsed first-72-hours process prevents the panicked missteps — destroyed
  evidence, premature public statements — that make breaches worse.

## 7. Common mistakes

- **"The cloud vendor is responsible."** You remain the controller; accountability
  does not transfer.
- **Public statement before counsel.** Early, wrong communication creates legal and
  reputational damage.
- **Assuming every breach must be notified — or that none must.** Scope and
  sensitivity decide; confirm with counsel.
- **Keeping data "just in case."** Minimisation and retention limits are legal
  duties.

## Put it to work

In the course you take a simulated healthcare data exposure, scope it from access
logs and cloud findings, assess obligations under Act 843, list the questions for
counsel, and produce a **breach assessment** and a **compliance gap register**
with named owners (`labctl worksheet m8`) — feeding the Module 9 board memo and
the Module 10 capstone's legal/privacy cell.

## Key takeaways

- **Act 843** = personal data (DPC); **Act 1038** = cybersecurity (CSA, CII,
  reporting). **GDPR** is the global benchmark (72-hour norm, DPIAs, big fines).
- You are almost certainly a **data controller** — register and comply.
- **Outsourcing processing never outsources accountability.**
- First 72 hours: **contain → scope → triage notification → counsel.**
- Notification depends on **scope + data sensitivity**; CII has mandatory
  reporting.

## Check yourself

1. **A cloud vendor misconfigures storage and leaks your customers' data. Who is
   accountable under Act 843?**
   → You — the data controller. The vendor is a processor; accountability does not
   transfer with the task.

2. **Ransomware encrypts your systems but you find no evidence of data theft. Is
   the notification question the same as a confirmed-exfiltration breach?**
   → No — the key question is whether data left your control. Encryption-only vs
   confirmed exfiltration are different; confirm specifics with counsel.

3. **First action in the first 72 hours — investigate or contain?**
   → Contain (stop active access) first — while preserving evidence — then
   investigate.

4. **What is the GDPR breach-notification norm, and why should a Ghanaian
   institution care?**
   → Notification to the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware; it sets the
   expectation partners, clients and regulators increasingly assume.

5. **Which Ghanaian body oversees personal data, and which oversees
   cybersecurity?**
   → The Data Protection Commission (Act 843) and the Cyber Security Authority
   (Act 1038).

## Glossary

- **Act 843** — Ghana Data Protection Act 2012 (personal data; DPC).
- **Act 1038** — Ghana Cybersecurity Act 2020 (CSA, CII, reporting).
- **GDPR** — the EU data-protection regulation; the global benchmark.
- **Data controller / processor** — decides how data is used / processes it for
  the controller.
- **CII** — Critical Information Infrastructure, designated under Act 1038.
- **DPIA** — Data Protection Impact Assessment for high-risk processing.
- **Data minimisation / retention** — collect only what's needed / keep only as
  long as needed.
- **CERT-GH** — national incident-response team under the CSA.
