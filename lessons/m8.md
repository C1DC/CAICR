# Module 8 — Data Protection, Privacy & Cybersecurity Compliance

**Day 4 · Afternoon · Leadership module (no Kali lab — you build a compliance assessment)**

In one sentence: in Ghana, protecting data and reporting incidents is not
optional goodwill — it is the law, and this module teaches you which laws apply,
what they require, and what to do in the first hours of a breach.

By the end you will be able to:
- apply the Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843) to real data handling;
- explain the Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038) and the CSA's powers;
- run the first 72 hours of a breach with the right triage decisions;
- know which questions require a lawyer before you answer them.

---

## The big picture

Two Ghanaian laws frame everything:

- **Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843)** — about *personal data*. It is overseen
  by the **Data Protection Commission (DPC)**. If you hold data about identifiable
  people — customers, citizens, staff — it applies to you.
- **Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038)** — about *cybersecurity*. It created the
  **Cyber Security Authority (CSA)**, licenses cybersecurity providers, designates
  **Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)**, and sets incident-reporting
  duties.

Act 843 rests on a set of **data-protection principles** — handle data lawfully
and for a stated purpose; collect only what you need (minimisation); keep it
accurate; keep it secure; keep it only as long as needed; and be **accountable**
for all of it. A practical rule of thumb: *if you hold personal data in Ghana,
you are a data controller — so register with the DPC, comply, and protect it.*

Two roles with different duties:

- **Data controller** — decides *why and how* data is processed (your
  institution). Carries the primary legal obligations.
- **Data processor** — processes it *on the controller's behalf* (e.g. a cloud
  vendor). Outsourcing the processing does **not** outsource your accountability.

Some data is **sensitive** — health, biometrics, financial, criminal records —
and demands heightened protection.

## An analogy

Think of personal data as cash you're holding *in trust* for other people. You
don't own it; you're a custodian. You must keep only what you were given reason
to hold, store it in a safe, not lend it out carelessly, return or destroy it
when the reason ends — and you can be audited on how you handled it. Hiring a
security van company (a processor) to move the cash doesn't end your
responsibility for it; if they lose it, *you* still answer to the owner.

## How it actually works

When a breach hits, the **first 72 hours** follow a disciplined order:

1. **Contain first, investigate second.** Stop the active access before analysis
   (but preserve evidence — see Module 6).
2. **Scope it.** What data, which systems, how many people, is the attacker still
   in?
3. **Triage notification.** Not every breach requires the same response; scope and
   data sensitivity drive the obligation.
4. **Involve legal counsel** *before* any external communication.

Notification obligations differ by breach type — a useful mental grid:

| Breach type | Immediate action | Notification question |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Ransomware, systems encrypted only | Isolate, preserve, restore from clean backup | Was data *exfiltrated*? If yes, notification may apply |
| Ransomware + confirmed data theft | Isolate, preserve, engage counsel now | Notification likely — scope & timing need legal advice |
| Phishing / credential compromise | Reset creds, revoke sessions, investigate access | What could that account reach? |
| BEC / financial fraud | Preserve email evidence, contact bank to recall funds | Financial recovery first; data exposure may be secondary |
| Cloud misconfiguration exposing data | Remove public access, then scope duration | Duration + sensitivity drive the obligation |

For **CII operators** (critical sectors), Act 1038 imposes **mandatory incident
reporting** to the CSA/CERT-GH with specific expectations — and because exact
timelines and formats can change, they are a matter to confirm with counsel, not
to guess.

## Ghana in focus

Picture a Ghanaian hospital that discovers a cloud repository of patient records
was left publicly accessible, with logs showing external downloads. Act 843 is
squarely engaged: patient health data is *sensitive*; the hospital is the *data
controller* even though a vendor ran the cloud; and the download logs mean this
isn't merely exposure but probable *exfiltration*. The leadership questions are
immediate — scope the exposure, remove public access, preserve the logs, and get
counsel to determine DPC notification. This is why the technical forensics of
Module 6 and the legal framework here are two halves of one response.

## Why it matters

- Non-compliance carries real consequences — DPC enforcement, CSA action, and the
  reputational hit of a public breach.
- Knowing your controller/processor status tells you *whose* responsibility a
  breach is (usually still yours).
- A rehearsed first-72-hours process prevents the panicked missteps — deleting
  evidence, premature public statements — that make breaches worse.

## Common mistakes

- **"The cloud vendor is responsible."** You remain the controller; accountability
  doesn't transfer.
- **Public statement before counsel.** Early, wrong communication creates legal
  and reputational damage.
- **Assuming every breach must be notified — or that none must.** Scope and
  sensitivity decide; confirm with counsel.
- **Keeping data "just in case."** Minimisation and retention limits are legal
  duties, not suggestions.

## Put it to work

In the course you take a simulated healthcare data exposure, scope the probable
exposure from access logs and cloud findings, assess obligations under Act 843,
list the questions for counsel, and produce a **compliance gap register** with
named owners plus an executive privacy brief — feeding the Module 9 board memo
and the Module 10 capstone's legal/privacy cell.

## Key takeaways

- **Act 843** = personal data (DPC); **Act 1038** = cybersecurity (CSA, CII,
  reporting).
- You are almost certainly a **data controller** — register and comply.
- **Outsourcing processing never outsources accountability.**
- First 72 hours: **contain → scope → triage notification → counsel.**
- Notification depends on **scope + data sensitivity**; CII has mandatory
  reporting.

## Check yourself

1. **A cloud vendor misconfigures storage and leaks your customers' data. Who is
   accountable under Act 843?**
   → You — the data controller. The vendor is a processor; your accountability
   doesn't transfer with the task.

2. **Ransomware encrypts your systems but you find no evidence of data theft. Is
   the notification question the same as a confirmed-exfiltration breach?**
   → No. Encryption-only vs confirmed exfiltration are different; the key question
   is whether data left your control, and the answer drives notification. Confirm
   specifics with counsel.

3. **What's the first action in the first 72 hours — investigate or contain?**
   → Contain (stop active access) first — while preserving evidence — then
   investigate.

4. **Which Ghanaian body oversees personal data, and which oversees
   cybersecurity?**
   → The Data Protection Commission (Act 843) and the Cyber Security Authority
   (Act 1038).

## Glossary

- **Act 843** — Ghana Data Protection Act 2012 (personal data; DPC).
- **Act 1038** — Ghana Cybersecurity Act 2020 (CSA, CII, reporting).
- **Data controller / processor** — decides how data is used / processes it on
  the controller's behalf.
- **CII** — Critical Information Infrastructure, designated under Act 1038.
- **Data minimisation** — collect and keep only what you need.
- **CERT-GH** — national incident-response team under the CSA.
