# Module 8 — Data Protection, Privacy & Cybersecurity Compliance

**Day 4 · Afternoon Session · ~3 hours · Hands-on case lab: `labctl start m8` · Deliverables: `labctl worksheet m8`**

In Ghana, protecting personal data and reporting serious incidents is not
goodwill — it is the law, with a regulator, powers and penalties behind it. This
module teaches you the principles and lawful bases that govern every use of
personal data, the rights people hold over it, how to run privacy by design
(DPIAs, data mapping, transfers), what to do in the first hours of a breach
— including the questions you must not answer without a lawyer — and how to build
a compliance programme that stands up to the DPC.

::: outcome | By the end of this module you will be able to

2. Determine whether you are a **data controller** or **processor**, run **vendor/third-party risk management (TPRM)**, and apply the cloud shared-responsibility model and the written **DPA**.
3. Build privacy in from the start — **Privacy by Design & by Default**, **data classification & lifecycle**, retention, and **PETs** (encryption, pseudonymisation/tokenization, anonymisation, differential privacy).

6. Run the **first 72 hours** of a breach, work the **notification decision tree**, and write a notification that contains what the law requires (documenting even when you don't notify).

## 1 · Two Laws, One Duty of Care

- **Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843)** governs *personal data*, overseen by the
  **Data Protection Commission (DPC)**. If you hold data about identifiable
  people — customers, citizens, staff — it applies to you.
- **Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038)** governs *cybersecurity*. It created the
  **Cyber Security Authority (CSA)**, licenses cybersecurity providers, designates
  **Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)**, and sets incident-reporting
  duties. **CERT-GH** is the national coordination centre under the CSA.

::: key | The practical rule
If you hold personal data in Ghana, you **are** a data controller. Register with
the DPC. Comply. Protect. "We're too small to matter" is not a legal position.
:::

### 1.1 The data-protection principles

Act 843 (and the GDPR) rest on a short set of **principles**. Each comes with a
practical test you can actually apply:

| Principle | Means | The test to apply |
|-----------|-------|-------------------|
| **Lawfulness, fairness, transparency** | process lawfully and tell people | Do we have a lawful basis *and* a privacy notice? |
| **Purpose limitation** | only for the stated purpose | Are we re-using it for something new? Then get a fresh basis |
| **Data minimisation** | only what you need | Why collect the Ghana Card for a newsletter sign-up? |
| **Accuracy** | keep it correct and current | Can people see and correct their record? |
| **Storage limitation** | keep only as long as needed | Is there a retention schedule — and do we actually delete? |
| **Integrity & confidentiality** | secure it | Encryption, access control — the controls of Modules 1–7 |
| **Accountability** | be able to *demonstrate* all the above | Could we show the DPC our records, DPIAs and training? |

### 1.2 Pick the right lawful basis

You may only process personal data if you have a **lawful basis** — and you choose
it *before* you start, not after a complaint. The main six:

- **Consent** — freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous and withdrawable.
  Bundled or forced "consent" (as in the Akoma case) is **not valid**.
- **Contract** — needed to deliver a service the person asked for.
- **Legal obligation** — a law requires it (KYC, tax, reporting).
- **Vital interests** — a life-or-death situation.
- **Public task** — a public authority's official function.
- **Legitimate interests** — your genuine need, *balanced* against the person's
  rights (not available to public bodies for their core tasks).

::: key | Consent is often the weakest choice
Teams reach for "consent" by reflex, but it can be **withdrawn** at any time and is
**easily invalid** if not freely given. Where a contract or legal obligation
actually fits, use that instead — you'll be on firmer ground.
:::

## 2 · Controller vs Processor — Accountability Doesn't Transfer

Two roles with different obligations:

- **Data controller** — decides *why and how* data is processed (your
  institution). Carries the primary legal liability.
- **Data processor** — processes it *on the controller's behalf* (e.g. a cloud
  vendor, a payroll bureau, an outsourced IT firm).

::: warning | Outsourcing the processing does not outsource the accountability
If your cloud vendor misconfigures a bucket and leaks your customers' data, **you**
— the controller — answer to the DPC and to the data subjects. The vendor is a
processor; your accountability does not transfer with the task. This is the single
most common misunderstanding among Ghanaian institutions moving to the cloud.
:::

**Sensitive data** — health, biometrics, financial records, criminal history —
demands heightened protection and makes a breach far more serious.

::: note | The custodian's mindset
Treat personal data as **cash you hold in trust**. You don't own it; you are a
custodian. Keep only what you were given reason to hold, store it in a safe, don't
lend it out carelessly, and return or destroy it when the reason ends — and expect
to be audited on how you handled it. Hiring a security-van firm (a processor) to
move the cash does not end *your* responsibility for it.
:::

### 2.1 The processor contract (DPA) and the cloud

Because accountability stays with you, the law requires a **written data
processing agreement (DPA)** with *every* processor — binding them to process only
on your instructions, to secure the data, to use **sub-processors** only with your
permission, to help you with breaches and data-subject requests, and to
return/delete the data at the end. A vendor relationship with no DPA (the Akoma
case) is itself a compliance failure.

For cloud specifically, know the **shared-responsibility model**: the provider
secures the *infrastructure*; **you** secure your *configuration, access and
data*. A misconfigured public storage bucket is always the **customer's** fault,
not the cloud's — which is why "we moved it to the cloud" is not a defence.

### 2.2 Third-party & vendor risk management (TPRM)

Most modern breaches arrive **through a vendor** (Target via an HVAC supplier,
Module 3; countless cloud leaks). Because accountability stays with you, managing
your processors is a core control, not paperwork:

- **Due diligence before you sign** — does the vendor have real security
  (certifications §8, a breach history, sub-processors you can live with)?
- **The DPA** (§2.1) — the binding contract; no DPA, no deal.
- **A processor / sub-processor register** — know *every* party that touches your
  data, including the vendor's vendors (the "fourth parties").
- **Least-privilege access** for vendors (Module 3), and **offboarding** — revoke
  access and confirm data return/deletion when the relationship ends.
- **Continuous monitoring** — security questionnaires, the right to audit, and
  watching for the vendor's *own* breaches.

::: key | Your attack surface includes everyone you share data with
You can run a flawless programme and still be breached through a careless
processor — and *you* still answer for it. Treat vendor risk as part of your own
risk register (Module 2): rank processors by the sensitivity of the data they hold
and the access they have, and concentrate your diligence on the top of that list.
:::

## 3 · Privacy by Design, Data Classification & PETs

Compliance isn't only reacting to breaches and requests — the durable work is
**building privacy in from the start** so there is less to go wrong and less to
lose.

**Privacy by Design & by Default** (a principle the GDPR makes a legal duty) means
privacy is engineered into a system from day one and the *default* setting is the
most protective one — not an afterthought bolted on, and not something the user has
to switch on. In practice: minimise what you collect, limit who can see it, set
retention to expire, and turn sharing *off* by default.

### 3.1 Classify your data, and manage its lifecycle

You cannot protect everything equally, so you **classify** — typically four tiers —
and apply controls proportionate to the tier:

| Tier | Example | Handling |
|------|---------|----------|
| **Public** | published rates, marketing | no restriction |
| **Internal** | org charts, internal memos | staff-only |
| **Confidential** | customer PII, contracts | access-controlled, encrypted |
| **Restricted / Sensitive** | health, biometric, card, criminal data | strict least-privilege, encryption, DPIA, logging |

Data then moves through a **lifecycle**, and each stage needs a control — the
stages teams most often neglect are the last two:

### 3.2 Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) — how to need less raw data

The strongest privacy control is to **not hold identifiable data** when you don't
need it. The techniques, from weakest to strongest de-identification:

- **Encryption** — protects data at rest/in transit, but it is still *personal
  data* (you hold the key); essential, not de-identification.
- **Pseudonymisation** — replace identifiers with a token/reference, keeping a
  separate re-identification key (e.g. **tokenization**, Module 5). Still personal
  data, but a breach of the pseudonymised set alone leaks less.
- **Anonymisation** — irreversibly strip identifiers so a person can no longer be
  singled out. Done properly, the data is **no longer personal data** and falls out
  of Act 843 — but true anonymisation is hard (re-identification via combination is
  a real risk).
- **Aggregation / differential privacy** — publish statistics, not records, with
  mathematical noise that bounds what any individual's data contributes.

::: key | Minimisation is the cheapest security control there is
Every field you *don't* collect, and every record you *do* delete on schedule, is
data that cannot be breached, subpoenaed, or mis-used. Privacy by Design and
storage limitation aren't just legal duties — they shrink the blast radius of
every incident in Modules 5–7.
:::

## 4 · Rights, DPIAs & Cross-Border Transfers

### 4.1 Data-subject rights and the DSAR

People hold rights over their own data: **access** (a *DSAR* — "send me a copy of
everything you hold on me"), **rectification**, **erasure** ("right to be
forgotten"), **restriction**, **portability**, **objection**, and rights around
**automated decisions** (Module 7). A **DSAR** must be answered on a timeline (the
GDPR norm is **one month**; confirm Act 843 specifics with counsel), usually free,
after verifying the requester's identity. Have a process — log it, find the data
(you need a data map), redact third parties, respond on time. Mishandled DSARs are
one of the most common complaints that reach a regulator.

### 4.2 DPIA and data mapping

You cannot protect what you don't know you hold. A **record of processing / data
map** — *what* data, *why*, *where* it lives, *who* it's shared with, *how long*
you keep it — is the foundation of everything else. A **DPIA (Data Protection
Impact Assessment)** is required **before** high-risk processing: large-scale
sensitive data, systematic monitoring, or new technology (including **AI**, Module
7). It forces you to weigh the risk to individuals and design mitigations *before*
you build. Skipping a required DPIA — as Akoma did with its sensitive health data —
is itself a breach of the Act.

### 4.3 Cross-border transfers and data residency

Sending personal data abroad — a foreign cloud region, an overseas processor, a
foreign **AI API** (Module 7) — needs a **lawful transfer mechanism** and attention
to where the data physically lives. Ghana's regime and sector rules (for example
**Bank of Ghana** directives on financial data) may require certain data to remain
**in-country**. Before you adopt any foreign service, answer: *where does our data
go, and are we allowed to send it there?*

## 5 · The Global & Sectoral Landscape — GDPR and Beyond

Even when it does not directly bind you, the EU **GDPR** sets the expectations
that clients, partners and regulators increasingly assume:

- a **72-hour** breach-notification norm (to the regulator, once aware);
- **data-subject rights**: access, rectification, erasure ("right to be forgotten"), portability;
- **Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)** for high-risk processing;
- privacy **by design and by default**;
- fines up to **4% of global turnover**.

::: case | GDPR enforcement shows the stakes
**British Airways** and **Marriott** drew multi-million-pound penalties after
breaches of customer data — the fines turned on *inadequate protection*,
independent of how sophisticated the attacker was. Regulators now punish the
failure to protect, not merely the fact of being attacked.
:::

Knowing GDPR tells you where the regulatory world is heading — and where Ghanaian
practice is expected to meet it.

### 5.1 Sectoral and regional regimes you will meet

Act 843 is the general law, but real organisations sit under **several overlapping
regimes** at once, by sector and by geography:

| Regime | Scope | Why it matters here |
|--------|-------|---------------------|
| **HIPAA** (US) | health data | the benchmark for health privacy — directly relevant to the Akoma case |
| **PCI-DSS** | cardholder data | contractual, not statutory, but mandatory to take cards (Module 5) |
| **Nigeria NDPR / NDPA** | personal data in Nigeria | the regional heavyweight; relevant to West-African operations |
| **South Africa POPIA** | personal data in SA | another maturing African regime |
| **AU Malabo Convention** | pan-African cyber & data | the continental framework Ghana aligns toward |
| **Sector rules (Bank of Ghana)** | financial data | may mandate in-country storage and specific reporting |

The practical takeaway: **map which regimes apply to each dataset** (a health
fintech serving Nigerian customers on a foreign cloud touches Act 843, HIPAA-style
expectations, PCI-DSS, NDPA *and* BoG rules at once) and comply with the
**strictest** obligation among them.

::: ghana | Data localisation is a rising requirement
Several regulators — including the **Bank of Ghana** for financial data — are
moving toward **data-residency / localisation** rules that require certain data to
be stored and processed **in-country**. Before adopting any foreign cloud region
or SaaS, confirm you are *allowed* to send that data there (§4.3).
:::

Knowing this landscape lets you answer the board's real question — "which rules
apply to us, and are we meeting the toughest one?"

1. **Contain first, investigate second** — stop active access (while preserving
   evidence: *isolate, don't power off*).
2. **Scope it** — what data, which systems, how many people, is the attacker still present?
3. **Triage notification** — scope and data sensitivity drive the obligation.
4. **Engage legal counsel** *before* any external communication.

Notification differs by breach type — a working mental grid:

| Breach type | Immediate action | Notification question |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Ransomware, systems encrypted only | Isolate, preserve, restore from clean backup | Was data *exfiltrated*? If yes, notification may apply |
| Ransomware + confirmed data theft | Isolate, preserve, engage counsel now | Notification likely — scope & timing need legal advice |
| Phishing / credential compromise | Reset creds, revoke sessions, investigate | What could that account reach? |
| BEC / financial fraud | Preserve email evidence, contact bank to recall | Financial recovery first; data exposure may be secondary |
| Cloud misconfiguration exposing data | Remove public access, then scope duration | Duration + sensitivity drive the obligation |

### 6.1 What a notification must contain — and documenting even when you don't notify

When you *do* notify, a vague "we had an incident" is not enough. A regulator
notification (GDPR Art. 33 is the template the world follows) states:

- the **nature** of the breach and the **categories and approximate numbers** of
  people and records affected;
- the **likely consequences** for those people;
- the **measures taken or proposed** to address it and mitigate harm;
- the **DPO / contact point** for follow-up.

The notification to the **affected people** (when high-risk) is written in **plain
language**: what happened, what data, what *they* should do (reset passwords, watch
for fraud — Module 5), and how to reach you.

::: key | Document the breach even when you decide not to notify
Accountability means you must be able to **show your working**. For *every*
personal-data breach — even the ones that don't meet the notification threshold —
record what happened, your risk assessment, and why you decided not to notify. If
the DPC later disagrees, "we assessed it and here is the documented reasoning" is a
defensible position; "we didn't think about it" is not. Keep a **breach register**.
:::

## 7 · CII and Mandatory Reporting (Act 1038)

Certain sectors are designated **Critical Information Infrastructure**. For CII
operators, Act 1038 imposes **mandatory incident reporting** to the CSA/CERT-GH,
with timelines and formats that a professional confirms against current CSA
directives rather than guessing. Reporting is also a *defensive* act: it lets the
CSA issue sector-wide warnings — which is exactly what organised-crime groups are
counting on you *not* to do.

## 8 · Standards, Certification & Assurance

Laws tell you *what* you must achieve; **standards and certifications** are how you
*demonstrate* it — to a regulator, a board, an auditor or a client doing vendor
diligence (§2.2) on *you*. They turn "trust us" into independently verified
assurance.

| Standard | What it certifies | Use it to |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **ISO/IEC 27001** | an Information Security Management System (ISMS) | prove a managed, audited security programme |
| **ISO/IEC 27701** | a Privacy Information Management System (extends 27001) | prove privacy governance (maps to GDPR/Act 843) |
| **SOC 2** (Type II) | security/availability/confidentiality controls operating over time | the report clients and partners ask SaaS vendors for |
| **PCI-DSS** | cardholder-data controls | mandatory to process card payments (Module 5) |
| **NIST CSF 2.0** | risk-based security outcomes (Govern→Recover) | the common language to organise it all (Module 2) |

These **map onto each other and onto the law** — an ISO 27001 ISMS, a 27701
privacy extension and your Act 843 obligations share most of the same controls, so
you build the control *once* and show it to each audience in their language. The
evidence you keep for an auditor — policies, the risk register (Module 2), DPIAs,
training records, the breach register (§6.1), the processor register (§2.2) — *is*
the "accountability" the data-protection principles demand.

::: key | Certification is evidence, not a force field
ISO 27001 or SOC 2 does **not** make you unbreachable — it proves you run a managed
programme. Its real power is **assurance and trust**: it lets regulators, boards and
customers verify your controls without taking your word, and it is increasingly the
*entry ticket* to enterprise and government contracts.
:::

---

## 9 · Building the Compliance Programme

Compliance is a **programme**, not a one-off document. A credible one rests on a
few pillars a regulator (and your board) will expect to see:

- **An accountable role** — a **DPO** / data-protection supervisor with authority
  and direct access to the board (bridges to Module 9's governance).
- **DPC registration**, kept **current** — not lapsed, as Akoma's was.
- **A record of processing + a retention schedule** — know what you hold, and
  delete on time (the storage-limitation principle made operational).
- **The core policies** — privacy notice, data-protection policy, retention policy,
  a **breach-response plan**, and an acceptable-use policy.
- **A DPA with every processor**, and a register of processors/sub-processors.
- **Training and awareness** — staff are the front line (Module 5), so this is a
  control, not a formality.
- **DPIAs** for every new high-risk processing activity.
- **Audit and a compliance calendar** — scheduled reviews of registration,
  retention runs, training and DPIAs, tested by internal audit (the three-lines
  model, Module 9).

::: key | A breach is a governance event, not just a technical one
Regulatory settlements, consumer redress and executive departures follow a serious
breach — so the **board**, not just the SOC, answers for it. That is why the
compliance programme is board-owned, and why this module hands straight to Module
9's governance.
:::

## 10 · Hands-On Practicals — Do These Yourself

Governance is a *doing* skill too. These short exercises produce the exact
artefacts a DPO maintains — practise them on your own organisation (or a realistic
imaginary one) before the graded Akoma case.

::: key | Practical 1 — classify a dataset & set retention (10 min)
Take a real system you know (a CRM, an HR file). List its data fields, assign each
a **classification** tier (§3.1), and write a one-line **retention period** per
category with a disposal method. *Win condition:* a mini data-classification +
retention table you could hand to IT.
:::

::: key | Practical 2 — write one ROPA entry (10 min)
Fill a **Record of Processing Activities** row for one activity: *purpose · data
categories · data subjects · lawful basis (§1.2) · recipients/processors · transfers
· retention*. *Win condition:* a complete, defensible ROPA line — the atom of a
data map.
:::

::: key | Practical 3 — a 5-question mini-DPIA (15 min)
For a new high-risk idea (e.g. AI that scores customers from health data, Module 7),
answer: *What data? What's the risk to people? Is it necessary & proportionate? What
mitigations? Residual risk — proceed, change, or stop?* *Win condition:* a go/no-go
recommendation with reasoning.
:::

::: key | Practical 4 — map the right lawful basis (10 min)
For five activities (newsletter, payroll, CCTV, KYC checks, loan scoring) pick the
**best lawful basis** (§1.2) and say why — and flag where "consent" would be the
*wrong* reflex. *Win condition:* five justified choices.
:::

::: key | Practical 5 — draft a breach-notification letter (15 min)
Using the §6.1 elements, write the **plain-language letter to affected people** for
a cloud-exposure of customer records: what happened, what data, what they should
do, how to contact you. *Win condition:* a letter a non-technical customer would
understand and act on.
:::

::: key | Practical 6 — a vendor-DPA checklist (10 min)
Build a 6-point checklist you'd run before signing any processor (§2.1–2.2): DPA in
place, security evidence/certification (§8), sub-processor list, data location,
breach-assistance clause, deletion-on-exit. *Win condition:* a reusable
onboarding checklist.
:::

## Key takeaways

- **Act 843** = personal data (DPC); **Act 1038** = cybersecurity (CSA, CII, reporting). **GDPR** is the global benchmark; **HIPAA/PCI/NDPA/POPIA/Malabo** and **data-localisation** rules may also apply — comply with the **strictest**.
- Every use of personal data needs a **lawful basis** and must honour the
  **principles** — minimisation, storage limitation, accountability. **Consent**
  is often the weakest choice.
- **Build privacy in:** Privacy by Design & by Default, **classify** data and manage
  its **lifecycle** (disposal is the forgotten stage), and use **PETs** —
  minimisation is the cheapest security control there is.
- You are almost certainly a **data controller** — register, sign a **DPA** with
  every processor, and run **vendor/third-party risk management**. **Outsourcing the
  task never outsources the accountability** (the misconfigured bucket is your fault).
- Honour **data-subject rights** (the **DSAR**); run a **DPIA** and keep a **data
  map**; watch **cross-border transfers**.
- First 72 hours: **contain → scope → triage → counsel**, then the **decision tree**
  (regulator; and data subjects if high risk). A notification must contain the
  required elements; **document even the breaches you don't notify** (breach register).
- **Prove it** with standards/certification (**ISO 27001/27701, SOC 2, NIST CSF**).
  Compliance is a **programme** (DPO, registration, retention, DPAs, training, audit)
  — and a **breach is a governance event the board answers for.**

## Check yourself

1. **A cloud vendor misconfigures storage and leaks your customers' data. Who is accountable under Act 843?**
   → You — the data controller. The vendor is a processor; accountability does not transfer with the task.

2. **Ransomware encrypts your systems but you find no evidence of data theft. Is the notification question the same as a confirmed-exfiltration breach?**
   → No — the key question is whether data left your control. Encryption-only vs confirmed exfiltration differ; confirm specifics with counsel.

3. **First action in the first 72 hours — investigate or contain?**
   → Contain (stop active access) first — while preserving evidence — then investigate.

4. **What is the GDPR breach-notification norm, and why should a Ghanaian institution care?**
   → Notify the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware; it is the expectation partners, clients and regulators increasingly assume.

5. **Which Ghanaian body oversees personal data, and which oversees cybersecurity?**
   → The Data Protection Commission (Act 843) and the Cyber Security Authority (Act 1038).

6. **A hospital recorded "consent" for its patient data, bundled into a mandatory
   intake form patients couldn't decline. Is that a valid lawful basis? Why?**
   → No — consent must be **freely given**, specific and unambiguous; bundled or
   forced consent is invalid. A contract or legal-obligation basis would likely fit
   better.

7. **Before launching an AI that profiles customers from their health records, what
   assessment is legally required, and what must you keep to even run it?**
   → A **DPIA** (the processing is high-risk, sensitive and uses new tech), and a
   **record of processing / data map**. Skipping a required DPIA is itself a breach.

8. **A likely personal-data breach poses *high* risk to the individuals affected.
   Who must you notify?**
   → **Both** — the regulator (DPC) within the deadline, **and** the affected data
   subjects directly, because the risk to them is high.

9. **What's the difference between pseudonymisation and anonymisation, and why does
   it matter legally?**
   → Pseudonymisation (e.g. tokenization) replaces identifiers but keeps a
   re-identification key — it is **still personal data**. True anonymisation is
   irreversible, so the data **falls outside Act 843**. Anonymise where you can; it
   removes the obligation and the breach risk together.

10. **A breach happens but you assess the risk to individuals as low and decide not
    to notify. What must you still do?**
    → **Document it** — the facts, your risk assessment and your reasoning — in a
    **breach register**. Accountability means being able to show the DPC your
    working if it later disagrees.

11. **Your SaaS startup keeps losing enterprise deals at the security-review stage.
    What would most change that, and why?**
    → A recognised **certification** (SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001) — it lets a
    customer's vendor-risk team verify your controls independently, and is often the
    entry ticket to enterprise/government contracts.

12. **A health fintech serves Nigerian customers from a foreign cloud. Which
    regimes might apply, and how do you decide what to follow?**
    → Potentially Act 843, Nigeria's **NDPA**, HIPAA-style health expectations,
    **PCI-DSS** for payments, and **BoG** localisation rules — **map which apply to
    each dataset and comply with the strictest** obligation.

## Glossary

- **Act 843** — Ghana Data Protection Act 2012 (personal data; DPC).
- **Act 1038** — Ghana Cybersecurity Act 2020 (CSA, CII, reporting).
- **GDPR** — the EU data-protection regulation; the global benchmark.
- **Data controller / processor** — decides how data is used / processes it for the controller.
- **Lawful basis** — consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests.
- **DPA (data processing agreement)** — the written controller–processor contract the law requires.
- **DSAR** — Data Subject Access Request: "send me a copy of everything you hold on me."
- **Data-subject rights** — access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, automated-decision rights.
- **DPIA** — Data Protection Impact Assessment, required before high-risk processing.
- **Record of processing / data map** — the inventory of what personal data you hold, why, where and for how long.
- **Cross-border transfer / data residency** — sending personal data abroad / rules on where it may physically live.
- **CII** — Critical Information Infrastructure, designated under Act 1038.
- **Data minimisation / storage limitation** — collect only what's needed / keep only as long as needed.
- **DPO** — Data Protection Officer: the accountable role for the compliance programme.
- **CERT-GH** — national incident-response team under the CSA.
- **Privacy by Design & by Default** — engineering privacy in from the start, with the most protective setting as default (a GDPR duty).
- **Data classification** — tiering data (public / internal / confidential / restricted-sensitive) to apply proportionate controls.
- **Data lifecycle** — create → store → use → share → archive → **destroy**; each stage needs a control (disposal is the neglected one).
- **PETs** — privacy-enhancing technologies: encryption, **pseudonymisation** (tokenization — still personal data), **anonymisation** (irreversible — no longer personal data), aggregation / **differential privacy**.
- **TPRM / sub-processor** — third-party (vendor) risk management / a processor's own processor, which you must know about.
- **ROPA** — Record of Processing Activities; the structured data map (purpose, categories, basis, recipients, transfers, retention).
- **Breach register** — the log of every personal-data breach and your notify/no-notify reasoning (accountability).
- **HIPAA / PCI-DSS / NDPA / POPIA / Malabo** — US health law / card-data standard / Nigeria's / South Africa's data law / the AU cyber & data convention.
- **Data localisation / residency** — rules requiring certain data to be stored/processed in-country.
- **ISO 27001 / ISO 27701 / SOC 2** — the ISMS standard / its privacy (PIMS) extension / the US controls-assurance report clients request.
