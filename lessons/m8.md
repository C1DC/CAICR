# Module 8 — Data Protection, Privacy & Cybersecurity Compliance

**Day 4 · Afternoon Session · ~3 hours · Hands-on case lab: `labctl start m8` · Deliverables: `labctl worksheet m8`**

In Ghana, protecting personal data and reporting serious incidents is not
goodwill — it is the law, with a regulator, powers and penalties behind it. This
module teaches you the principles and lawful bases that govern every use of
personal data, the rights people hold over it, how to run privacy by design
(DPIAs, data mapping, transfers), what to do in the first hours of a breach
— including the questions you must not answer without a lawyer — and how to build
a compliance programme that stands up to the DPC.

2. Determine whether you are a **data controller** or **processor**, and why the written **DPA** and the cloud shared-responsibility model matter.
3. Handle **data-subject rights** — including a **DSAR** — and run a **DPIA** and a data map for high-risk processing.
4. Judge **cross-border transfer / data-residency** questions (foreign cloud, foreign AI APIs).
5. Explain **Act 843** (DPC) and **Act 1038** (CSA, CII, reporting), and situate Ghana against the **GDPR** benchmark.
6. Run the **first 72 hours** of a breach and work the **notification decision tree** (regulator and/or data subjects).
7. **Build a compliance programme** — DPO, DPC registration, retention schedule, DPAs, training, audit.
8. Produce a **breach assessment** and a **compliance-gap register** (the Akoma Health case lab + worksheets).

## 1 · Two Laws, One Duty of Care

- **Data Protection Act 2012 (Act 843)** governs *personal data*, overseen by the
  **Data Protection Commission (DPC)**. If you hold data about identifiable
  people — customers, citizens, staff — it applies to you.
- **Cybersecurity Act 2020 (Act 1038)** governs *cybersecurity*. It created the
  **Cyber Security Authority (CSA)**, licenses cybersecurity providers, designates
  **Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)**, and sets incident-reporting
  duties. **CERT-GH** is the national coordination centre under the CSA.

::: key | The practical rule
If you hold personal data in Ghana, you **are** a data controller. Register with
the DPC. Comply. Protect. "We're too small to matter" is not a legal position.
:::

### 1.1 The data-protection principles

Act 843 (and the GDPR) rest on a short set of **principles**. Each comes with a
practical test you can actually apply:

| Principle | Means | The test to apply |
|-----------|-------|-------------------|
| **Lawfulness, fairness, transparency** | process lawfully and tell people | Do we have a lawful basis *and* a privacy notice? |
| **Purpose limitation** | only for the stated purpose | Are we re-using it for something new? Then get a fresh basis |
| **Data minimisation** | only what you need | Why collect the Ghana Card for a newsletter sign-up? |
| **Accuracy** | keep it correct and current | Can people see and correct their record? |
| **Storage limitation** | keep only as long as needed | Is there a retention schedule — and do we actually delete? |
| **Integrity & confidentiality** | secure it | Encryption, access control — the controls of Modules 1–7 |
| **Accountability** | be able to *demonstrate* all the above | Could we show the DPC our records, DPIAs and training? |

### 1.2 Pick the right lawful basis

You may only process personal data if you have a **lawful basis** — and you choose
it *before* you start, not after a complaint. The main six:

- **Consent** — freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous and withdrawable.
  Bundled or forced "consent" (as in the Akoma case) is **not valid**.
- **Contract** — needed to deliver a service the person asked for.
- **Legal obligation** — a law requires it (KYC, tax, reporting).
- **Vital interests** — a life-or-death situation.
- **Public task** — a public authority's official function.
- **Legitimate interests** — your genuine need, *balanced* against the person's
  rights (not available to public bodies for their core tasks).

::: key | Consent is often the weakest choice
Teams reach for "consent" by reflex, but it can be **withdrawn** at any time and is
**easily invalid** if not freely given. Where a contract or legal obligation
actually fits, use that instead — you'll be on firmer ground.
:::

## 2 · Controller vs Processor — Accountability Doesn't Transfer

Two roles with different obligations:

- **Data controller** — decides *why and how* data is processed (your
  institution). Carries the primary legal liability.
- **Data processor** — processes it *on the controller's behalf* (e.g. a cloud
  vendor, a payroll bureau, an outsourced IT firm).

::: warning | Outsourcing the processing does not outsource the accountability
If your cloud vendor misconfigures a bucket and leaks your customers' data, **you**
— the controller — answer to the DPC and to the data subjects. The vendor is a
processor; your accountability does not transfer with the task. This is the single
most common misunderstanding among Ghanaian institutions moving to the cloud.
:::

**Sensitive data** — health, biometrics, financial records, criminal history —
demands heightened protection and makes a breach far more serious.

::: note | The custodian's mindset
Treat personal data as **cash you hold in trust**. You don't own it; you are a
custodian. Keep only what you were given reason to hold, store it in a safe, don't
lend it out carelessly, and return or destroy it when the reason ends — and expect
to be audited on how you handled it. Hiring a security-van firm (a processor) to
move the cash does not end *your* responsibility for it.
:::

### 2.1 The processor contract (DPA) and the cloud

Because accountability stays with you, the law requires a **written data
processing agreement (DPA)** with *every* processor — binding them to process only
on your instructions, to secure the data, to use **sub-processors** only with your
permission, to help you with breaches and data-subject requests, and to
return/delete the data at the end. A vendor relationship with no DPA (the Akoma
case) is itself a compliance failure.

For cloud specifically, know the **shared-responsibility model**: the provider
secures the *infrastructure*; **you** secure your *configuration, access and
data*. A misconfigured public storage bucket is always the **customer's** fault,
not the cloud's — which is why "we moved it to the cloud" is not a defence.

## 3 · Rights, DPIAs & Cross-Border Transfers

### 3.1 Data-subject rights and the DSAR

People hold rights over their own data: **access** (a *DSAR* — "send me a copy of
everything you hold on me"), **rectification**, **erasure** ("right to be
forgotten"), **restriction**, **portability**, **objection**, and rights around
**automated decisions** (Module 7). A **DSAR** must be answered on a timeline (the
GDPR norm is **one month**; confirm Act 843 specifics with counsel), usually free,
after verifying the requester's identity. Have a process — log it, find the data
(you need a data map), redact third parties, respond on time. Mishandled DSARs are
one of the most common complaints that reach a regulator.

### 3.2 DPIA and data mapping

You cannot protect what you don't know you hold. A **record of processing / data
map** — *what* data, *why*, *where* it lives, *who* it's shared with, *how long*
you keep it — is the foundation of everything else. A **DPIA (Data Protection
Impact Assessment)** is required **before** high-risk processing: large-scale
sensitive data, systematic monitoring, or new technology (including **AI**, Module
7). It forces you to weigh the risk to individuals and design mitigations *before*
you build. Skipping a required DPIA — as Akoma did with its sensitive health data —
is itself a breach of the Act.

### 3.3 Cross-border transfers and data residency

Sending personal data abroad — a foreign cloud region, an overseas processor, a
foreign **AI API** (Module 7) — needs a **lawful transfer mechanism** and attention
to where the data physically lives. Ghana's regime and sector rules (for example
**Bank of Ghana** directives on financial data) may require certain data to remain
**in-country**. Before you adopt any foreign service, answer: *where does our data
go, and are we allowed to send it there?*

## 4 · The Global Benchmark — GDPR

Even when it does not directly bind you, the EU **GDPR** sets the expectations
that clients, partners and regulators increasingly assume:

- a **72-hour** breach-notification norm (to the regulator, once aware);
- **data-subject rights**: access, rectification, erasure ("right to be forgotten"), portability;
- **Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)** for high-risk processing;
- privacy **by design and by default**;
- fines up to **4% of global turnover**.

::: case | GDPR enforcement shows the stakes
**British Airways** and **Marriott** drew multi-million-pound penalties after
breaches of customer data — the fines turned on *inadequate protection*,
independent of how sophisticated the attacker was. Regulators now punish the
failure to protect, not merely the fact of being attacked.
:::

Knowing GDPR tells you where the regulatory world is heading — and where Ghanaian
practice is expected to meet it.

1. **Contain first, investigate second** — stop active access (while preserving
   evidence: *isolate, don't power off*).
2. **Scope it** — what data, which systems, how many people, is the attacker still present?
3. **Triage notification** — scope and data sensitivity drive the obligation.
4. **Engage legal counsel** *before* any external communication.

Notification differs by breach type — a working mental grid:

| Breach type | Immediate action | Notification question |
|-------------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Ransomware, systems encrypted only | Isolate, preserve, restore from clean backup | Was data *exfiltrated*? If yes, notification may apply |
| Ransomware + confirmed data theft | Isolate, preserve, engage counsel now | Notification likely — scope & timing need legal advice |
| Phishing / credential compromise | Reset creds, revoke sessions, investigate | What could that account reach? |
| BEC / financial fraud | Preserve email evidence, contact bank to recall | Financial recovery first; data exposure may be secondary |
| Cloud misconfiguration exposing data | Remove public access, then scope duration | Duration + sensitivity drive the obligation |

## 6 · CII and Mandatory Reporting (Act 1038)

Certain sectors are designated **Critical Information Infrastructure**. For CII
operators, Act 1038 imposes **mandatory incident reporting** to the CSA/CERT-GH,
with timelines and formats that a professional confirms against current CSA
directives rather than guessing. Reporting is also a *defensive* act: it lets the
CSA issue sector-wide warnings — which is exactly what organised-crime groups are
counting on you *not* to do.

## 7 · Building the Compliance Programme

Compliance is a **programme**, not a one-off document. A credible one rests on a
few pillars a regulator (and your board) will expect to see:

- **An accountable role** — a **DPO** / data-protection supervisor with authority
  and direct access to the board (bridges to Module 9's governance).
- **DPC registration**, kept **current** — not lapsed, as Akoma's was.
- **A record of processing + a retention schedule** — know what you hold, and
  delete on time (the storage-limitation principle made operational).
- **The core policies** — privacy notice, data-protection policy, retention policy,
  a **breach-response plan**, and an acceptable-use policy.
- **A DPA with every processor**, and a register of processors/sub-processors.
- **Training and awareness** — staff are the front line (Module 5), so this is a
  control, not a formality.
- **DPIAs** for every new high-risk processing activity.
- **Audit and a compliance calendar** — scheduled reviews of registration,
  retention runs, training and DPIAs, tested by internal audit (the three-lines
  model, Module 9).

::: key | A breach is a governance event, not just a technical one
Regulatory settlements, consumer redress and executive departures follow a serious
breach — so the **board**, not just the SOC, answers for it. That is why the
compliance programme is board-owned, and why this module hands straight to Module
9's governance.
:::

## 8 · Scenario — Exposure and Consequence

::: ghana | Healthcare data exposure
A Ghanaian hospital discovers a cloud repository of patient records left publicly
accessible, with logs showing external downloads. Act 843 is squarely engaged:
the data is *sensitive* (health); the hospital is the *controller* even though a
vendor ran the cloud; and the download logs mean this is probable *exfiltration*,
not mere exposure. The immediate steps — remove public access, preserve the logs,
scope the exposure, and get counsel on DPC notification — are the fusion of
Module 6's forensics and this module's law. This is the exact scenario behind your
breach-assessment deliverable.
:::

::: case | Equifax aftermath
Beyond the technical failure, the aftermath — regulatory settlements, consumer
redress, executive departures — showed that a breach is a *legal and governance*
event, not only a technical one. The board, not just the SOC, answers for it.
:::

## Key takeaways

- **Act 843** = personal data (DPC); **Act 1038** = cybersecurity (CSA, CII, reporting). **GDPR** is the global benchmark.
- Every use of personal data needs a **lawful basis** and must honour the
  **principles** — minimisation, storage limitation, accountability. **Consent**
  is often the weakest choice.
- You are almost certainly a **data controller** — register, and sign a **DPA**
  with every processor. **Outsourcing the task never outsources the accountability**
  (the misconfigured cloud bucket is always your fault).
- Honour **data-subject rights** (the **DSAR**); run a **DPIA** and keep a **data
  map** for high-risk processing; watch **cross-border transfers**.
- First 72 hours: **contain → scope → triage → counsel**, then the **notification
  decision tree** (regulator; and data subjects if high risk).
- Compliance is a **programme** — DPO, DPC registration, retention schedule, DPAs,
  training, audit — and a **breach is a governance event the board answers for.**

## Check yourself

1. **A cloud vendor misconfigures storage and leaks your customers' data. Who is accountable under Act 843?**
   → You — the data controller. The vendor is a processor; accountability does not transfer with the task.

2. **Ransomware encrypts your systems but you find no evidence of data theft. Is the notification question the same as a confirmed-exfiltration breach?**
   → No — the key question is whether data left your control. Encryption-only vs confirmed exfiltration differ; confirm specifics with counsel.

3. **First action in the first 72 hours — investigate or contain?**
   → Contain (stop active access) first — while preserving evidence — then investigate.

4. **What is the GDPR breach-notification norm, and why should a Ghanaian institution care?**
   → Notify the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware; it is the expectation partners, clients and regulators increasingly assume.

5. **Which Ghanaian body oversees personal data, and which oversees cybersecurity?**
   → The Data Protection Commission (Act 843) and the Cyber Security Authority (Act 1038).

6. **A hospital recorded "consent" for its patient data, bundled into a mandatory
   intake form patients couldn't decline. Is that a valid lawful basis? Why?**
   → No — consent must be **freely given**, specific and unambiguous; bundled or
   forced consent is invalid. A contract or legal-obligation basis would likely fit
   better.

7. **Before launching an AI that profiles customers from their health records, what
   assessment is legally required, and what must you keep to even run it?**
   → A **DPIA** (the processing is high-risk, sensitive and uses new tech), and a
   **record of processing / data map**. Skipping a required DPIA is itself a breach.

8. **A likely personal-data breach poses *high* risk to the individuals affected.
   Who must you notify?**
   → **Both** — the regulator (DPC) within the deadline, **and** the affected data
   subjects directly, because the risk to them is high.

## Glossary

- **Act 843** — Ghana Data Protection Act 2012 (personal data; DPC).
- **Act 1038** — Ghana Cybersecurity Act 2020 (CSA, CII, reporting).
- **GDPR** — the EU data-protection regulation; the global benchmark.
- **Data controller / processor** — decides how data is used / processes it for the controller.
- **Lawful basis** — consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests.
- **DPA (data processing agreement)** — the written controller–processor contract the law requires.
- **DSAR** — Data Subject Access Request: "send me a copy of everything you hold on me."
- **Data-subject rights** — access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, automated-decision rights.
- **DPIA** — Data Protection Impact Assessment, required before high-risk processing.
- **Record of processing / data map** — the inventory of what personal data you hold, why, where and for how long.
- **Cross-border transfer / data residency** — sending personal data abroad / rules on where it may physically live.
- **CII** — Critical Information Infrastructure, designated under Act 1038.
- **Data minimisation / storage limitation** — collect only what's needed / keep only as long as needed.
- **DPO** — Data Protection Officer: the accountable role for the compliance programme.
- **CERT-GH** — national incident-response team under the CSA.
